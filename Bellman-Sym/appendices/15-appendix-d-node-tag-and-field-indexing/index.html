    Appendix D — Nodes and field indexing¶

    Summary

    A statement's equations determine its mathematical meaning; !node marks a statement as an authored node, a computation that can be named and addressed separately. Perch marks identify fields in their information context, while other bracket selections require a declared collection or family. This appendix explains how these distinctions preserve composition in both the field diagram and the functional diagram that Appendix C reads.

    Relationship with other documents

    Formal account — Fields, functions and types: field and functional diagrams; Operators and builders: operations within a block. Builds on — Appendix C: the declared context, substitution, and the conditions for interpreting equations as functions or relations; Perch tagging rules: the information sets of fields. Used by — Methodization: numerical methods address the computations identified here.

    1 What the marks describe¶

    The rules concern a .bl stage file. A typing context collects the declared domains, roles, perches, the typed arguments of the declared functions, and shock laws. A perch is an information set, and a field is a random variable measurable at its declared perch. Representing that field by a function of the declared state requires the sufficiency assumptions of Appendix C §1.2 and R5.

    Three pieces of syntax answer different questions:

    • A block's header, with its typed inputs and outputs, states which quantities it relates. Its equations determine the relation or operator.
    • An annotation such as :: [id=shift, !node] names a statement and marks it as an authored node: a computation to inspect or realize as a unit within the block.
    • A perch mark such as [>] identifies a field at continuation. It does not supply a state space, a representative function, or a numerical grid.

    For example, this transition fragment has one real input and two real outputs. The intermediate result y is also a declared output:

    [id=transform, (a : ℝ) : !dcsn -> (y : ℝ, q : ℝ) : !cntn] {
    y = 2 + a    :: [id=shift, !node]
    q = 4*y      :: [id=scale]
}

    The header identifies a decision-to-continuation transition. Renaming transform or shift does not change that transition. The !node on the first line keeps that line as a unit of its own; the addition and multiplication are mathematical operations whether that mark is present or absent.

    The statement mark is !node, written inside the annotation after ::. Methods remain in a separate methods file, and neither a block identifier nor a method name selects the block's mathematical formula.

    2 What an equation determines¶

    An explicit definition such as y = 2 + a gives a function when its arguments, domains, and dependencies are established. In §1, with \(a\in\mathbb R\), its representative is \(\mathrm g:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\), \(\mathrm g(a)=2+a\). The field equation and the function equation are related as Appendix C specifies; they are not the same kind of object.

    An implicit equation instead states a relation. For example, suppose the stage declares \(a=w-c\) and the continuation candidate \(c_\succ=\mathrm f(a)\). The policy equation c = c[>] then gives $$ {(w,c):c=\mathrm f(w-c),\ (w,c)\text{ is feasible}}. $$ It defines a policy only where a unique solution, or a specified selection from its solutions, meets the required measurability conditions. Adding !node makes the relation separately addressable; it proves neither existence nor uniqueness and does not make a root an optimizer (Appendix C R8 and R10).

    An alias is an explicit definition of an intermediate quantity that can be substituted at its uses. Substitution must preserve local binding, domains, shock dependence, and any retained control arguments. An implicit condition cannot be used as an assignment before its unknowns are determined. An equation defining a block output must continue to determine that output even if its expression is also substituted elsewhere.

    A statement marked !doc is documentation and contributes no defining equation. Consequently, adding !doc can remove a condition from the mathematical problem; it is not equivalent to adding or removing !node. An identifier alone changes neither kind of statement.

    3 Authored nodes and operator occurrences inside equations¶

    There are three levels of detail within the diagrams of a stage:

    1. A block is represented as a unit with its declared inputs and outputs.
    2. A statement marked !node specifies a unit within that block.
    3. The mathematical operations inside each equation remain available at finer detail, including their arguments and local binding.

    A computational statement is an authored node exactly when it carries !node. An operator occurrence is a particular use of an operation within an expression. An expectation, argmax, solve, upper_env, or an authored evaluate remains an operator occurrence even when its statement is unmarked. It does not thereby make that statement an authored node.

    For example, this arrival-value fragment contains two expectations and no authored nodes:

    [id=dcsn_to_arvl, (V) : !dcsn -> (V[<] : ℝ @ !vlu) : !arvl] {
    V[<]      = E_{y}(V)          :: [id=value]
    D_{b}V[<] = E_{y}(R*D_{w}V)   :: [id=marginal]
}

    The surrounding stage supplies the transition w = R*b + y from the arrival state b to the decision state w, the law of y, the return parameter R, and the decision value field. The second line is a derivative line: it states the derivative of the arrival value in b, with the factor R that the transition contributes. Both integrations belong to the mathematical interpretation, subject to Appendix C R7. A numerical method can address them without adding !node to either line.

    Arithmetic and the applications of declared functions also remain, within the equations that a block or a statement marked !node carries. Being mathematically represented does not make every operation a separate place: a place is an operator at which a methods file can type a method, which the stage lists with its path and its kind (Methodization §2; §6).

    These three levels occur within both the field diagram and the functional diagram, and replace neither. Both sides of an implicit equation and the rule relating the two diagrams must remain available; a printed formula or a method attachment cannot replace those mathematical objects.

    4 Composition and substitution¶

    In §1, let \(\mathrm g(a)=2+a\) and \(\mathrm h(y)=4y\), both maps from \(\mathbb R\) to \(\mathbb R\). The second output is $$ \mathrm q(a)=\mathrm h(\mathrm g(a))=4(2+a). $$ The whole transition has type \(\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\times\mathbb R\) and is $$ \mathrm F(a)=\bigl(\mathrm g(a),\mathrm h(\mathrm g(a))\bigr). $$ Substituting 2 + a for y in the second equation preserves \(\mathrm F\). Deleting the first output would not: the block promises both y and q. Keeping shift as a node records the intermediate computation while the composite function remains \(\mathrm F\).

    Composition requires matching types. For general maps \(\mathrm g:A\to B\) and \(\mathrm h:B\to C\), the composite \(\mathrm h\circ\mathrm g:A\to C\) is defined because the output of \(\mathrm g\) is an admissible input to \(\mathrm h\). If the second operation also needs a shock or another state argument, that argument must be retained; a node does not supply it.

    A node also does not hide dependencies. If y depends on a shock, substitution must retain that dependence. An expectation integrates over that shock only when its stated binding and law require it; other free arguments remain free. The formal arguments of functions and operator-bound variables keep their own scope. A perch mark binds no variable; a statement identifier binds no mathematical argument (Appendix C R2, R4, R5, and R11).

    Denotation means the mathematical object represented by an expression or diagram. For the same typed equations, adding or removing !node preserves their mathematical denotation while changing the recorded nodes: the marked statement's result becomes a function that later statements consume and a method may represent exactly or by an approximation. An operation occurring inside an unmarked statement also returns a function of the remaining arguments, but that function is not an authored object: it is obtained exactly through its definition and admits only the operation's own methods, never a representation (Appendix C R7, the result of an occurrence). #functional-semantics The diagrams therefore need not be identical. Likewise, equality of mathematical functions does not guarantee identical floating-point results after different numerical methods are attached.

    Technical details

    The substitution argument concerns equal denotations on the required domain, with local binding preserved. Almost-sure equality under one state law alone does not justify substituting representatives at states reached under a different policy or initial law. The distinction and the required representative choices are stated in Appendix C R5.

    5 Field marks, collection selection, and function arguments¶

    5.1 Perch marks¶

    V[<], V, and V[>] identify the arrival, decision, and continuation value fields. V[~] is an explicit spelling of the decision field V. For other names, the complete declarations and headers establish the native perch. A non-native marked occurrence, such as c[>], needs a definition or supplied interpretation at that perch; it is not automatically equal to the decision control c.

    The state at a perch can have several coordinates. A field marked [>] may therefore be represented by a function of a vector, not necessarily one scalar asset. Its arguments follow from the declared state and the equations, under the sufficiency assumptions of Appendix C. The mark alone establishes no factorization through a particular coordinate.

    5.2 Collection and family selection¶

    Brackets other than the perch marks require an appropriate collection or family. For a tuple, positions start at one: (y, q)[1] selects y. A named selector must resolve to a declared key or to a selector with a compatible domain. For example, the retirement choice uses

    V = (V_w[>] - δ, V_r[>])[d]    :: [id=choice_value]

    Here the surrounding declaration gives d the ordered labels {work, retire}. work selects the first expression and retire the second. Each expression must have the required value type. The tuple of alternative values is distinct from the labeled union of successor state spaces (Appendix C R3 and §4.7).

    An indexed collection may have a parameter-defined number of entries. With an integer parameter J, L_J := {1, …, J} and δ : L_J → ℝ, a name j : L_J selects the entry δ[j]; dist : L_J × L_J → ℝ₊ is selected as dist[j][i] when both names have suitable declared domains. The selector can be a state, a control or a parameter. Its declaration must establish membership in the index set; declaring it only on ℕ does not do so. Symbol conventions §9.2.1 states the admitted index sets and how calibration associates keys with entries.

    For a product-valued field, q[>][1] first identifies the continuation field and then selects its first component. Put the perch mark before component selectors. An integer literal in brackets selects a component by position; it is not an inter-period time shift.

    A name in brackets does not declare an information set

    The spellings q[a] and q[y] do not by themselves declare that q is a function of the state a or the intermediate value y. They require an established indexed object and the appropriate selection rule. They neither bind the bracketed name in an equation's right side nor provide missing domains or measurability assumptions. A state can select an entry when its declared domain supplies suitable keys, as above. To apply a declared function to a state, use parentheses, 𝑞(a) (§5.3).

    A table key may be a marked input, such as the age in wage_fn[age[>]][tau]. The forward header names that input. The key's value identifies a row in the declared table, independent of the row's storage position. In a backward equation, an indexed value inherits the dependence of its key.

    5.3 Explicit function arguments¶

    Use a declared function when the intended mathematical map and its arguments should be explicit:

    𝑔(a : ℝ) := 2 + a
𝑞(y : ℝ) := 4*y

    The second declaration defines a function on \(\mathbb R\); its formal argument y is local to that function. In a field equation, 𝑞(y) applies the function to the field y, while 𝑞(𝑔(a)) writes the composition directly. Neither application changes the field's declared perch.

    Suppose \(q_A:A\to Q\) is a function on a state space \(A\), with value set \(Q\), and \(g:A\to Y\) is an intermediate map. Representing \(q_A\) using only that intermediate value requires \(q_Y:g(A)\to Q\) with \(q_A=q_Y\circ g\). Such a function exists exactly when \(g(a_1)=g(a_2)\) implies \(q_A(a_1)=q_A(a_2)\). To construct it, set \(q_Y(g(a))=q_A(a)\); the condition makes the result independent of the chosen \(a\). Conversely, the composition forces equal \(g\) values to give equal \(q_A\) values. Measurability of \(q_Y\) remains a separate requirement. A bracket spelling cannot establish that equation. Appendix C R10 uses the same condition when candidate parameters represent a policy.

    6 Addresses and numerical methods¶

    An address identifies a declared block, statement, or operation for inspection or method attachment. The address says where to attach; the typed equations say what computation is being approximated. In the examples above:

    • transform addresses the whole transition, and transform.shift addresses the statement marked !node.
    • dcsn_to_arvl.value.E_y addresses the expectation in the named value equation. The statement's id=value identifies that occurrence; it does not make the statement a node.
    • A stage-wide E_y address applies to the occurrences of expectation over y covered by that methods interface. A statement-qualified address distinguishes the integration in the value line from the one in the derivative line when they need different attachments.

    Use explicit statement identifiers for stable addresses. A line number is not a method address: inserting documentation must not redirect an attachment. Renaming identifiers consistently in the stage and its methods file preserves the equations and the intended attachments. Identifiers must be unambiguous in their enclosing scope.

    An address used for inspection is not necessarily a place. An unmarked scalar alias can remain identifiable without defining a separate numerical task. An authored node, an operator occurrence and the Evaluation of a builder are places of different kinds, and typing a method requires a place of a kind the method admits and a method suitable for its mathematical operation (Method types).

    The address <block-id>.<statement-id>.<operator> must identify the intended occurrence. If a statement contains two indistinguishable occurrences under that address, the attachment is ambiguous; it must not silently choose the first. A stage-wide attachment, where supported, is an explicit choice to govern the corresponding group.

    Methods and their settings are written separately from the stage. A quadrature rule approximates an integral; a root finder approximates a solution of the stated relation. Neither supplies a missing shock law, constraint, or optimality condition. A numerical grid is part of a chosen representation, whereas the field's domain belongs to the model: the methods file writes the grid by its own entry at the place, cntn_to_dcsn.evaluate.grid : !Cartesian(m_d=(m_min, m_max, n_m)), one factor for each coordinate, and the method by its basis, such as !Linear (see Methodization §3.2).

    The name evaluate. An authored evaluate(knots, values, x) denotes evaluation of a stated representation, with coordinates first and values second. The numerical interpolation and extrapolation must be specified as Appendix C R7 requires. It is an operation inside its line, with declared arguments, and a method attaches to it at its own address, <block-id>.<statement-id>.evaluate, where a representation needs no grid, since the first argument holds the knots; evaluate alone names the one authored occurrence of a stage and is refused where the stage authors none or several. A methods file also writes evaluate after a block id for the Evaluation that closes the builder, the place of a declared output representation, and may say which stored object it types: policy.evaluate for the policy function of the policy builder, cntn_to_dcsn.evaluate for everything the Evaluation of a backward value builder stores, the value function and the derivative of it that a derivative line states, or cntn_to_dcsn.evaluate[V] and cntn_to_dcsn.evaluate[D_{m_d}V] for each of them apart, with a grid of its own. That place names an operation every such builder closes with; it does not assert that an evaluate expression was written in the stage, and it creates none. A representation typed on the builder itself is refused under method-admission. See Methodization §2 for the written form.

    7 Policy relations and numerical decomposition¶

    The policy block of the consumption stage cons of Appendix C §4.2, written in the interior form with its candidate line an equation, provides an example in which an unmarked statement is a relation rather than an alias:

    [id=policy, (V[>] : [-inf, inf) @ !vlu) : !cntn -> (c : ℝ₊₊ @ !ctrl) : !dcsn] {
    c[>]   = 𝑑𝑢𝑖𝑛𝑣(D_{a}V[>])    :: [id=InvEuler]
    m_d[>] = a + c[>]             :: [id=endoggrid]
    c      = c[>]                 :: [id=cons_choice]
}

    The surrounding cons stage declares the transition \(a=m_d-c\) and the inverse marginal-utility function. Its continuation value, and so the derivative D_{a}V[>] that the candidate line reads, is already discounted; no discount factor should be inserted into this fragment. Appendix C §4.2 derives its functions and §4.5 states sufficient conditions and a constrained extension; the methods file applications/portfolio-choice/declaration/stages/cons/methods.bl, beside cons.bl, writes the candidate line as the inequality c[>] <= 𝑑𝑢𝑖𝑛𝑣(D_{a}V[>]) ⟂ a >= 0, whose clause states the borrowing constraint (Appendix C §4.5).

    The first two lines define the candidate and its decision coordinate. The third identifies the decision control with the candidate. Read at the decision perch, it determines the control implicitly through the transition; rewritten at the continuation perch, through the coordinate map of the second line, it states the policy at the cash on hand of each saving, the relation the construction states (Appendix C R5 and §4.3). It remains a relation even though it has no !node mark and contains no written solve operator: the policy builder, the operator the policy block denotes, holds it as an operation whose output is its own unknown. A method may address it as a solution, and the methods interface records a solve place for it; that recognition does not turn it into an authored solve expression or erase either side of the equation.

    The endogenous grid method approximates this relation by computing candidate pairs and representing the resulting policy, which the pairs determine under Appendix C R10's consistency, single-valuedness, coverage, and measurability conditions. When the final line is omitted, no line states the policy and the block is refused under the rule output-defined (Appendix C R1 and §4.6); an implementation does not invent a missing final equation or a place from the names of the lines.

    Choosing a numerical method does not add evaluate, upper_env, or !node statements to the model. If a method internally interpolates or compares candidate values, those computations realize the stated problem. An authored upper_env is instead a mathematical operation in its own right, with the selection and attainment requirements of Appendix C R12. A method's internal steps may be displayed for inspection, but must remain distinguishable from the authored equations and their nodes.

    8 Compositional meaning¶

    The compositionality requirement is that the meaning of an expression is determined by the meanings of its parts. For a language with names, those meanings retain their declared context, free arguments, and local binding. An operator's interpretation can then combine them without guessing what a statement identifier was intended to mean. This is the denotational discipline described by Hutton (2023, §3), applied here to the field equations and typed operations of Appendix C.

    It permits substitution of equal mathematical meanings within a valid context. It does not equate every diagram recording those meanings: an annotated diagram also records authored addresses and nodes. Removing a !node mark can preserve the represented mathematical function or relation while changing the diagram available for method attachment.

    Technical details

    A syntactic sort is a category of expressions. For an ordinary many-sorted signature, which lists constructors together with their input and output sorts, the freely generated terms form an initial algebra. Initial means that there is a unique map preserving those constructors into each specified algebra of the same signature (Goguen, Thatcher, Wagner, and Wright 1977, §2, Proposition 2.1). The target must already specify the mathematical sets and operations. The theorem does not choose missing interpretations, solve implicit equations, or prove their analytic assumptions. Binding and substitution require a representation that accounts for contexts; a many-sorted list of constructor arities alone is not a proof that those binding rules are respected (Lamiaux and Ahrens 2024, §1.1.1).

    The formal implementation must preserve these distinctions when it constructs the two diagrams. This appendix specifies their mathematical meaning and authored nodes; it does not prescribe parser constructors, storage records, or an execution order for a numerical solver.
