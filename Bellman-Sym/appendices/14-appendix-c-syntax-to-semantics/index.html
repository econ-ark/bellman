    Appendix C — From stage syntax to Bellman-calculus semantics¶

    Summary

    This appendix states the rules by which the equations of a stage file determine the mathematical objects of the Bellman calculus: which random variable each written name denotes and at which information set, which function of the state represents it, and how those functions compose into the stage's backward and forward operators. The rules are stated over the written expressions and the declared context, not over any parser or program, so that an economist can follow them by hand from a .bl file to the composed functions. Twelve rules, R1 to R12, state the account, and §4 applies them to complete stage files.

    Relationship with other documents

    Formal account — Stages and perches: perches as information sets and the almost-sure function representation; Fields, functions and types: the field diagram and the functional diagram; Operators and builders: the primitive operators the rules name. Builds on — Stage syntax, Equation parsing rules, and Perch tagging rules: what may be written in a stage file. Used by — Appendix D: the !node mark and the addressing of statements; Methodization: numerical methods attach to the operators these rules produce. See also — Operations: the kinds Pullback, Aggregation, Expectation, Argmax and Evaluation of the operations a builder holds, which R7 and R8 name.

    1 What the rules produce¶

    1.1 Stages, perches, and fields¶

    A stage is one step of a Bellman problem, read as a passage through three states of knowledge. The agent arrives, learns what the pre-decision shock reveals, decides, and continues, learning what the post-decision shock reveals. The three states of knowledge are the perches: arrival, written \(\prec\); decision, written \(\sim\); continuation, written \(\succ\). Formally each perch is a \(\sigma\)-algebra on one underlying probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P)\), and the three are nested, \(\mathcal F_\prec \subseteq \mathcal F_\sim \subseteq \mathcal F_\succ\), because the agent only ever learns more (Stages and perches §3).

    A field is a random variable on \(\Omega\) that takes values in its declared set and is measurable with respect to the \(\sigma\)-algebra of its perch. Every name a stage file declares with a set and a perch is a field: a state coordinate, a control, a value. The derivative of a value is no field: a line reads it by the term D_{a}V[>], the derivative of the continuation value in the continuation coordinate a (§1.5, R8). A parameter is classified as such by the complete context, including the block headers; it is constant with respect to the stage uncertainty. A shock is a random variable supplied on an edge between two perches. Its law is declared on a line of its own, y ~ …, and an expectation over a shock that has no declared law is refused under not-constructed. The shock is drawn on one edge, the edge of the forward block whose header lists it (Shock distributions §1). A second forward block that lists it is refused under distribution-edge. A later law of motion therefore reads the realized shock through a state that the earlier law of motion defines, such as y_d = y. The shock is available at the later perch and is not assumed known earlier. This does not rule out a degenerate shock that is already measurable earlier.

    One name may be read at two perches. In a stage file w denotes cash on hand at the decision perch and w[>] denotes cash on hand at the continuation perch; these are two fields, two random variables, and they are equal only when a statement says so. The perch marks [<], [~], and [>] name the arrival, decision, and continuation perches; a field at the decision perch is written bare, and x[~] is the same field as x (Perch tagging rules). The package reads x[~] as the bare name x wherever it is written, and it does not yet check that the name is at the decision perch (§5.3). On a backward line the package does not yet read the bare name of a decision state when a state at another perch has the same name: the line is refused under name-in-scope, so such a decision state takes a name of its own, such as w_d (§5.3).

    1.2 The state at a perch and the function representing a field¶

    The state vector \(X_p\) collects the declared state coordinates at perch \(p\), in their declared order, and takes values in the state space \(\mathsf X_p\). These are different objects: \(X_p\) is a random variable, whereas \(\mathsf X_p\) is its set of possible values. A state space may be a product or a labeled union of branch spaces.

    To represent a field \(Z\) by a function of \(X_p\), require that \(Z\) be measurable with respect to the information generated by \(X_p\), up to null sets. Measurability at the perch \(\mathcal F_p\) alone is insufficient if \(X_p\) omits relevant information. The state must also determine the conditional laws needed for the stage. These are the relevant state-sufficiency assumptions; they do not assert that the current state reconstructs every earlier observation. The package does not yet check these assumptions (§5.3).

    For real-valued fields, and more generally standard Borel value spaces, this gives a measurable representative \(\mathrm z\) with \(Z = \mathrm z(X_p)\) almost surely. A standard Borel space is a measurable space equivalent to a Borel subset of a complete separable metric space; real domains and their finite products are examples. The functional representation is the Doob–Dynkin lemma (Kallenberg 1997, Lemma 1.13). Representatives agree almost everywhere under the law of \(X_p\); the lemma does not determine their values at states that have zero probability under that law.

    Thus a field \(Z\), its representative function \(\mathrm z\), and the value \(\mathrm z(x)\) at a specified state are distinct. A decision control chosen as a measurable function of the decision state satisfies \(c = \pi(X_\sim)\), where \(\pi\) is its policy. Before choosing a policy, the control is retained as an argument of the transition and choice value, \(\mathrm g_\succ(x,c)\) and \(Q(x,c)\). Their definitions on all feasible state-control pairs cannot be recovered solely from observations under one fixed policy. R5 states the additional domain requirement.

    1.3 The typing context and the two graphs¶

    The typing context \(\Xi\) is the collection of everything the declarations and block headers of a file assert: for each name its set, its role, and its perch; for each declared function its formal arguments and its body; for each shock its law; for each block its inputs, its outputs, and the perches on its two sides. The letter is \(\Xi\) because \(\Gamma\) names a feasible set in this documentation, as in Constraints. Every rule below has the form under \(\Xi\), the expression \(e\) denotes the object such-and-such; nothing about an expression is decided from its spelling alone, and nothing is decided from the identifier a statement or block carries.

    The rules describe two related diagrams, the field diagram and the functional diagram of the calculus, which the package constructs as the two initial diagrams and the two builders graphs of a stage (semgraph). In the field diagram, inputs and outputs are typed fields and each operation relates the quantities in its context. An implicit equation remains a relation rather than a directed assignment to an already determined output. In the functional diagram, state arguments, pullback along the law of motion, and operators on functions are explicit. Both sides of each final equation remain available in both diagrams, with the source equations and rules that justify their correspondence. A pullback along the law of motion may use several field equations.

    A function is not always an input value of the same type as an evaluated field. For example, a transition has type \(\mathrm g_\succ : \mathsf X_\sim \times \mathsf C \to \mathsf X_\succ\), restricted to feasible pairs, while a backward operator has type \(B_\succ : \mathcal V_\succ \to \mathcal Q\). Here \(\mathcal V_\succ\) is the chosen class of continuation functions and \(\mathcal Q\) the class of choice-value functions of state and control. Evaluating a member of \(\mathcal V_\succ\) at a state is a further operation.

    In a categorical reading, an arrow has a specified input and output type; composition connects matching types and preserves identities and order. Products combine inputs, whereas a labeled union describes alternatives (R3). Describing two diagrams does not by itself establish a functor, a map between categories preserving identities and composition. R5 supplies a conditional interpretation, with state choices, representative functions, and analytic assumptions stated explicitly. It does not assert that the field and functional diagrams are identical or that either recovers a unique source spelling. See Fields, functions and types.

    The backward operator \(\mathbb T\) is read from the value and policy operations. The forward operator \(\mathbb M_\pi\) uses the transitions, laws, and a specified policy. Their exact relationship is given in R9.

    1.4 The two written forms¶

    A value statement can be written in two forms. In the perch form the names denote fields and carry no state arguments:

    V[<] = E_{y}(V)

    In the functional form the state arguments are explicit:

    \[ \mathrm v_\prec(b) = \int \mathrm v_\sim(y + R\,b)\,\mu_y(\mathrm dy). \]

    A stage file writes the perch form, and the rules derive the functional form from it under the context \(\Xi\); §4.4 carries one derivation through in full. Where a functional-form spelling is admitted, it is read under the same context and must determine the same functional diagram. The displayed integral assumes \(w=y+Rb\) and the conditional law specified in §4.4. The perch form names fields; functional notation makes their representatives and arguments explicit. A perch label alone does not change a random variable into a function or an evaluated number (§1.2).

    2 Syntax and the typing context¶

    2.1 Declarations¶

    A declaration is a name, a colon, and a type; a type is a set, a role word prefixed by !, or a conjunction of them, written with @ between them in any order. Several sets in one type declare their intersection, as in β : (0,1) @ (0, R^(ρ-1)) (Symbols and conventions §9.2). Several sets are read on the declaration of one name on a line of its own, outside the blocks. They are sets of numbers, none of them a set of labels or a product, and at most one of them is not written as an interval with two endpoints, such as [1, inf). The glyphs ℝ₊ and ℝ₊₊ count here as sets not so written, and two equal sets count as one: θ : ℤ @ [1, inf) is read, and θ : ℝ₊₊ @ ℤ is refused under syntax. Two intervals with no number in common, (0,1) @ (2,3), are refused under the same rule. The package decides this only between intervals whose endpoints are numbers or ratios of two integers. Where the sets have no number in common and one of them is a reserved set, a named set, a list of integers, a union or a difference, as in β : ℝ₊₊ @ (-2,-1), or an endpoint is a formula, as in (2*1, 3), it reads the declaration, and no calibration can bind the name (§5.3). A field listed in a block header carries one set, and several sets there are refused under readable-form.

    β : (0,1)
w : ℝ₊
c : !ctrl @ ℝ₊
V : ℝ @ !vlu
y : Y
y ~ LogNormal(μ_y, σ_y)
Xm := ℝ₊₊
𝑢(c : ℝ₊₊) := c^(1-ρ)/(1-ρ)

    These are declaration forms, not a complete stage: the surrounding file supplies Y, μ_y, σ_y, ρ, and the field perches. The complete context distinguishes the parameter β from the state coordinate w and classifies the controls and values.

    A derived parameter is a constant that a definition without arguments writes from the declared parameters, h := 1/(1 - Γ/R) (Symbol conventions §8). Its definition may declare the set its value lies in, on the same line: q : (0,1) := (β*R)^(1/ρ)/R. Calibration computes the value and refuses parameter values at which it lies outside that set. A separate declaration q : (0,1) beside the definition q := … is refused under one-name-one-meaning. A definition that declares a set and whose value reads no name, ρ : ℝ := 2, is refused under readable-form, since the calibration supplies that number. The set that a definition declares is a set of numbers, and any other set there is refused under derived-parameter.

    The definition of a derived parameter may call log and exp, each of one argument, and min and max, each of one argument or more; a call of any other function, or of log or exp with several arguments, is refused under derived-parameter, or under not-constructed where a call of another function is the whole right side. The definition may also be written by cases, with or without a declared set: h := if Γ < R then 1/(1 - Γ/R) else 0 and q : (0, 3) := if β < R then 1 else 2 are both read (Equation parsing rules §2 gives the forms of the test and of the branches). The conditional if … then … else … stands as the whole right side of a definition without arguments or as a branch of another conditional. Inside a formula, in a function's body, in a line of a block and as an endpoint of a set it is refused under syntax. The words if, then and else are no names: a file that writes one of them as a name, in a declaration, a definition, a binder, a block header or a formula, is refused under syntax. The package does not yet refuse one written as the id of a line's annotation, :: [id=if] (§5.3). In a derived parameter's definition log, exp, min and max are the functions of the language, and a definition that calls one of them where the stage defines a function of that name is refused under derived-parameter. A test reads what a branch reads, parameters, derived parameters and finite numbers: a set, a field or a function written there is refused under derived-parameter, and a label of a finite set under well-typed, as a label in arithmetic is.

    The value of a derived parameter is its definition evaluated at the calibration. For a definition by cases it is the branch that the test chooses, and the other branch is not evaluated, so that the value of h above is 0 at Γ = R. A branch written inf or -inf gives that infinity, and no arithmetic of infinities is defined: a definition that reads a name valued at an infinity where it is evaluated, in a formula, a call or a test, as the branch that its test chooses or as its whole right side, has no value, and the calibration is refused under derived-parameter at that definition. A branch written inf or -inf is admitted only in a definition whose declared set can hold that infinity, as ℝ_{+∞}, ℝ̄ and (0, inf] hold inf. A definition whose declared set cannot hold it is refused under derived-parameter when the file is read. The set ℝ, the declared set of a definition that writes none, holds no infinity, so h := if Γ < R then 1/(1 - Γ/R) else inf is refused and h : ℝ_{+∞} := if Γ < R then 1/(1 - Γ/R) else inf is read. Whether a set can hold the infinity is judged from its form, also for several sets in one type and for a named set written with a bar: ℝ̄ @ (0, inf] can hold inf, and ℝ̄ @ (0, inf), ℝ_{+∞} @ (0, 100] and {x : ℝ̄ | x < 100}, which exclude it by an open endpoint, by an endpoint that is a number and by a condition, cannot. A condition x <= e excludes inf only where e is a number, so ℝ_{+∞} @ (0, 2*100] is read and calibration refuses its infinite value, while (0, 2*100] is refused when the file is read. A union can hold an infinity that one of its members can hold. A difference is judged by its left side alone, so a definition on ℝ̄ \ [0, inf] is read, and calibration refuses its infinite value. Where the answer depends on a calibrated value, as for (0, T], the file is read, and calibration holds the value, finite or infinite, to the declared set, as it holds the value of every definition that declares a set.

    The declared set of a parameter may be a union or a difference of sets of numbers. ρ : (0,1) \/ (1, inf) declares that ρ lies in one of the two intervals, and ρ : ℝ₊₊ \ {1} that ρ is positive and is not 1; A ∪ B and A ∖ B are second spellings of A \/ B and A \ B. A union and a difference are read in the declared set of a parameter, of a derived parameter and of a setting, which a methods file declares (Methodization §4). They are refused in the set of a field, of a shock and of a function's argument, and they are not read after :=, in the definition of a named set or of a set-valued function such as Γ(x : ℝ₊) := (0, x].

    The set of a parameter or of a derived parameter reads parameters, derived parameters, named sets and numbers, and the set of a setting may also read settings. A derived parameter's set that reads a setting is refused under derived-parameter. A shock is read inside an expectation over it where its law reads no field. A set that reads a field, whether directly, through a named set or through the law of a shock that an expectation in it integrates, is refused under readable-form. An interval of numbers holds at least one number, so (2, 1) is refused under readable-form when the file is read. A difference holds at least one number as well. One whose left side lies within its right side, as in (0,1) \ ℝ₊, is refused under readable-form when the file is read, where the package can decide the inclusion. A difference that is a member of a union is not judged.

    An endpoint of an interval, or a side of a condition after the bar of a comprehension, {x : ℝ | x < T}, that is the bare name of a parameter or of a derived parameter valued at an infinity is read as that infinity, whatever supplies the value. With T : ℝ̄ calibrated at inf, the set [0, T) is \([0, \infty)\), and so is the set of debt : ℝ₊ @ [0, h) where the definition of h above, declared on ℝ_{+∞}, gives h the value inf. A bound of a parameter's set may call log, exp, min and max, which calibration evaluates as in a derived parameter's definition. A bound that calls another function is not decided, also where it computes with an infinity: calibration binds the value, and stage.obligations() lists its membership in the set as required. A bound that calibration evaluates and whose arithmetic has no value at the calibration, 1/(R-2) at R = 2, refuses the calibration, and so does one that computes with a name valued at an infinity, as 2*T and -T do at T = inf, or with the written infinity, as 2*inf does at every calibration.

    A declared function binds its formal arguments: the c in the body of 𝑢 is the function's own variable and not the control c of the stage (R4). The operators D_{c} and Inv applied to a declared function denote its derivative with respect to the named argument and its functional inverse, under the assumptions R7 records. The subscript of D_{c} names one scalar argument of the function: a subscript that names several arguments is refused under derivative-one-name, and one that names a product-valued argument under derivative-scalar-coordinate. The package does not yet check that the subscript names an argument of the function (§5.3).

    A named set may also be a comprehension, H := {i : ℤ | i >= 0, i <= 1}, whose bound name i is local to the definition, as a formal argument is local to its function. Marked !categories, a comprehension over ℤ, or over ℕ with a lower bound of at least 1, whose conditions bound the name below and above by integers written as numbers gives its members category names (Symbol conventions §§9.2–9.3):

    HouseholdStatus := {i : ℤ | i >= 0, i <= 1} :: [!categories household_status, newborn=0, old=1]

    The declaration denotes the set \(I = \{0, 1\}\) and its category map \(m_I : I \to C\), with \(m_I(0) = (\kappa, \mathrm{newborn})\) and \(m_I(1) = (\kappa, \mathrm{old})\), a bijection onto the set \(C = \{(\kappa, \mathrm{newborn}), (\kappa, \mathrm{old})\}\) of the categories the annotation names. Here \(\kappa\) is the identifier written after the marker, household_status, or, where none is written, the pair of the SHA-256 digest of the stage text and the set's name, so that two stage texts share categories only through an identifier. A field \(n : \Omega \to I\) declared on the set takes integer values, and the random variable \(m_I \circ n\) is its category. Arithmetic on the codes has its usual meaning, since every function \(f\) of the code is the function \(f \circ m_I^{-1}\) of the category composed with \(m_I\), so that the term 100 - 120*n is 100 for a newborn household and \(-20\) for an old one; the coding, however, defines no distance between the categories and gives no meaning to a number between two codes. A category name is no term of the file, so n_next = old is refused under name-in-scope, and the transition is written with the code, n_next = 1.

    A stage expectation requires a conditional law relative to its information context. When the law statement supplies that conditional law, dependence on earlier state fields must be retained. A constant conditional law implies independence from that earlier information. A marginal distribution with constant numerical parameters alone does not imply independence. The examples below explicitly use the constant-conditional-law interpretation. A tuple subject, (Ψ, θ) ~ N(…), declares a joint law as one factor. Separate declarations on one edge define the joint law of its shocks as the product of the declared conditional laws, each given its named parent shocks and the source coordinates: parent-free factors are conditionally independent given the source coordinates, and two children of one random parent need not be. The elaboration records the factorization as an assumption.

    2.2 Blocks and their headers¶

    A block is a bracketed header followed by statements:

    [id=cntn_to_dcsn, (V[>] : ℝ @ !vlu, c) : !cntn -> (V : ℝ @ !vlu) : !dcsn] {
    V      = 𝑢(c) + β*V[>]          :: [id=value]
    D_{w}V = 𝑑𝑢(c)                  :: [id=marginal]
}

    The word after a side names the information set of the coordinates listed on that side, with two exceptions. A shock listed on a side is an edge argument, not a state coordinate of that side. Its eventual measurability follows its timing (§1.1). A control is at the decision perch wherever it is listed, because a control is chosen with the decision information; listing it on a continuation side records that it is available there, which it is, since \(\mathcal F_\sim \subseteq \mathcal F_\succ\). A side may omit its perch word only when every non-shock coordinate it lists is placed without it: a control is at the decision perch, and any other coordinate carries the mark [<] or [>] or a perch word in its own type, as in (w : !dcsn, c : !ctrl @ ℝ₊₊) -> (a : ℝ₊) : !cntn. A bare name is not placed by another block's listing of it; the perch of a name is never guessed. A perch mark on a name must agree with what the context already records, and a mark [<] or [>] that disagrees with the perch word of its side is refused under field-perch. The package does not yet check the mark [~], which it reads as the bare name (§5.3). Sets written for the same name at several places are intersected as described in §2.4. Where one block writes a field and another reads it, the set written must lie in the set read (§2.4).

    The second line of the block is a derivative line: its left side is the derivative term D_{w}V, the derivative of the value V the block writes in the state coordinate w of its output perch, and the line states that derivative (R8). It adds no field to the header and no output to the block.

    The identifier after id= is an address. It names the block for the methods file and for error messages, and it decides nothing about the block's meaning (R1).

    2.3 Statements and annotations¶

    A statement is an equation, optionally followed by :: and a bracketed list of annotations. [id=value] gives the statement an address. A dotted id, [id=optimality.euler], instead attaches the statement to the line optimality of its policy block as a property of that line: the statement forms no operation and takes no address of its own (Constraints §4.1). A dotted id is refused under readable-form where its block writes no control, or where the line it names is absent, is marked !doc or is itself a property. !doc marks a statement as documentation. It defines no equation of the block's operator, but its content may be retained as a condition. The binder of a documentary maximization also declares the feasible set of its control, which enters the control's type. !node marks a statement as an authored node, so that the function it defines is an operator of its own for a method to address (Appendix D). The mark needs an id in the same annotation, :: [id=disc, !node]; without one the statement is refused under declared-once. The marker !categories is written only in the annotation of a named set of the form of §2.1; on a statement, a block header or any other item it is refused under category-codes. The clause ⟂ m_d - c >= 0 after a decision-perch equation of a policy block gives a complementarity condition with a specified residual sign (R10), using the law of motion a = m_d - c. It is not unchanged when the equation's two sides are exchanged. After an equation the clause may carry two bounds on one quantity, ⟂ 0 <= c_p <= w_d; after an inequality it carries one. The body of a policy block may also declare a multiplier, μ[>] : ℝ₊ ⟂ a >= 0: a name with the continuation mark, one declared set and a clause with one bound. The declaration defines no equation; it adds a coordinate at the continuation perch to the functions its block defines there (R1, R10). A clause and a multiplier are written in a policy block. The package does not yet check this: in another block it reads them, and they enter no rule of that block's builder (§5.3).

    2.4 Forming the context¶

    The context is formed before any statement is read. Every declaration and every block side contributes what it asserts about each name; a name's set is the intersection of the sets asserted for it, so c : !ctrl @ ℝ₊ on one side and c : ℝ₊₊ @ !ctrl on another give the control the set \(\mathbb R_{++}\). The package computes the intersection of sets that are written identically, of the sets ℝ, ℝ₊ and ℝ₊₊, and of intervals whose endpoints are numbers or infinity. It does not yet compute the intersection of other sets, such as a named set Z := ℝ₊₊ beside ℝ₊₊, and it then records no set for the name (§5.3).

    Where one block writes a field and another reads it, the set written must lie in the set read. The policy block may write c : ℝ₊₊ @ !ctrl for a transition that reads c : !ctrl @ ℝ₊. With the two sets exchanged, the reading block declares the narrower set, and the stage is refused under set-mismatch. The package does not yet check the inclusion where it cannot decide it, as with an endpoint that names a parameter, or where the control stands on the left side of a line marked !doc (§5.3). A field declared on a set with category codes (§2.1) is declared on that set at every place that writes a set for it, and another set at one of them is refused under category-codes.

    Two assertions that cannot both hold, such as two perches for one field (field-perch) or two roles for one name (one-name-one-meaning), are an error. Sets written for one field that have no value in common are such assertions. The package does not yet check this, except between a block that writes the field and a block that reads it (§5.3). A name whose set is asserted nowhere is an error, not a parameter: a parameter exists only where the file declares one. The package does not yet check this for a field or a shock that a block header lists (§5.3); a name that a line reads and that the file declares nowhere is refused under name-in-scope. A name that the file declares with a set on a line of its own, and that no block header lists, is a parameter, and a line of a block that writes it is refused under parameter-or-field.

    2.5 Binders, free names, and bound names¶

    The formal arguments of functions and operator subscripts determine the local variables of an expression. E_{y} integrates over the designated shock, while argmax_{c} and solve_{c} specify a control or unknown within their arguments. The binders of argmax and solve stand, in order, for the fields that the left side of the line writes, and a binder may be a new name, as cp is in c = argmax_{cp : Γ(w)}(𝑢(cp) + β*V[>]). The left side's own names written in another order are refused under not-constructed. Nested local variables refer to their innermost binder. D_{c}(𝑢) identifies the formal argument of the declared function that is differentiated; it does not bind unrelated occurrences of the stage control. The package does not yet check that the subscript names a formal argument of the function (§5.3).

    Two kinds of dependence must be distinguished. The integration variable introduced by an expectation is local. Its integrand's declared field dependencies may include that shock indirectly: if w = y + b*R, the field V represented by \(\mathrm v_\sim(w)\) depends on y through w. R5 exposes this dependence before R7 integrates it. An independently bound parameter with the same spelling is not that shock. Expanding a function must substitute its arguments without capturing a different local variable.

    A name free relative to an operator is not bound by that operator. It may still be random. In expectation over Y, quantities R and K remain free unless another rule binds them. Holding them fixed in a conditional integral requires the appropriate conditional law; it does not establish that they are constant or independent of Y.

    3 The rules¶

    Each rule states the written form it governs, what the context supplies, the object the form denotes, and the assumptions under which the denotation holds or the condition under which the form is rejected. A rejection may identify conflicting declarations, an undefined operation, or a construction for which the required context has not been supplied. An analytic assumption is a condition needed for a stated interpretation; it is recorded separately from the information that defines the model. The reference page Rules and their code lists, for each rule, the functions of the package that implement it and check it, and the refusals that the package raises when a file breaks it. Where the package does not yet check a rule, this appendix says so at the rule, and §5.3 lists each such rule.

    R1 Blocks and their headers¶

    Written form. A block [id=B, (inputs) : !p -> (outputs) : !q] { … } with its statements, as in §2.2.

    Context. The header contributes the sets, roles, and perches of its coordinates (§2.4). The statements are read in order (R2). A multiplier declared in the body of a policy block (§2.3) contributes one more name, at the continuation perch, with the role of a multiplier and its declared set. It is a coordinate of the functions the policy block defines at that perch and of no other function: it is no input or output of the block or of any other block, no header lists it, and the functions the block reads keep their own coordinates (R10).

    Interpretation. A block denotes a typed relation between its inputs and outputs. Explicit definitions give an operator; an implicit system gives one only after the required solution or selection conditions are supplied. Under R5 the block can denote an operator between the functions representing those fields. What kind of operator it is follows from the header and the roles of its outputs, never from its identifier:

    Header Kind Denotes
    a state at \(\prec\), possibly with a shock, to a state at \(\sim\) forward transition \(\mathrm g_\prec\), and the forward operator \(\mathbb M_\prec\) (R9)
    a state at \(\sim\) with controls, possibly with a shock, to a state at \(\succ\) forward transition \(\mathrm g_\succ\), and \(\mathbb M_{\succ,\pi}\) (R9)
    value fields at \(\succ\) (with controls) to value fields at \(\sim\); a state at \(\sim\) is a coordinate, while a state at \(\succ\) is reached through the law of motion backward block the builder at a policy \(\mathbb T_{\succ,\pi}\), which carries the continuation value function to the decision value function, \((\mathbb T_{\succ,\pi}\mathrm v_\succ)(x) = Q(x, \pi(x))\), where \(Q = B_\succ(\mathrm v_\succ)\) is the choice value that the induced aggregator \(B_\succ\) gives on state and control; the block's lines are read at the control that the policy gives, and where the policy block writes a maximization, the choice value on state and control is its objective (Stages and perches §2; Operators and builders §6; R5, R7, R8)
    value fields at \(\sim\) to value fields at \(\prec\); a state at \(\prec\) is a coordinate, while a state at \(\sim\) or \(\succ\) is reached through the law of motion backward block the arrival aggregator \(B_\prec\) (R5, R7)
    value fields at \(\succ\) to controls at \(\sim\); the lines read the derivatives of those values by derivative terms such as D_{a}V[>], which the header does not list; a state at \(\sim\) is a coordinate, while a state at \(\succ\) is reached through the law of motion, apart from candidate lines indexed by continuation state policy block the policy \(\pi\) (R8, R10)

    A header is the rank of the symbol its block adds to the stage signature, its input sorts and its output sort (The stage term §1). The backward symbols have ranks over the value-function sorts alone, so a backward header lists the value fields it reads and writes, with the controls at which its lines are read, and no state: the derivative of a value is read by the term D_{a}V[>] and is no field of a header. A shock may also be listed, as on any side (§2.2). The listing adds no input to the block's builder. It lets a line read, outside an expectation, a shock drawn on the edge between the block's two perches, where a later line of the block integrates it, as in W = η*V[>] followed by V = 𝑢(c) + E_{η}(W). A state of the perch at which the block's output stands is a coordinate of the output function and is not listed; a state of its input perch is reached only through the law of motion (R5, R7). The reason is the one Fields, functions and types §1.2 gives: a value field at a perch is measurable with respect to that perch's state, so every coordinate of the state is already an argument of the output, and a state of another perch is not. A header that lists a state writes a rank the signature has no symbol for, and the term algebra holds only the terms they admit (Initial-algebra semantics §1); it is refused under header-coordinate rather than read with the state removed, since read by the rule that a header's inputs are the builder's wires it would assert a wire of state sort that no builder produces, and the written header would no longer determine the rank. A control is listed because the lines are read at it: the block denotes the operator at the policy that represents the control (R8), whose output is the value function on the decision state alone, and the control remains an argument only of the choice value that the written equation gives (R5) and of the law of motion. Forward headers list the arguments of the law of motion, a map between state spaces.

    A direct read of an input or later perch's state on any backward line is refused, including a read in a relation or a condition after ⟂: under input-perch-state where no forward block between the two perches draws a shock, and under shock-observed where one does. A read under an expectation over the edge's shock is admitted, as is the continuation-state indexing of a policy candidate line (R8, R10).

    Two blocks with the same header and the same statements denote the same operator whatever their identifiers; renaming every identifier consistently throughout a file and its methods file changes no denotation. Changing a header or a statement can.

    Within a block, statements define outputs, introduce intermediate quantities, or impose implicit relations (R10). An output statement defines an output of the block. An alias is an operator-free statement whose left side is neither an output nor the unknown of an implicit statement (R10); it names an intermediate quantity and is substituted into the statements that follow (R2). A statement marked !doc is documentation. A statement marked !node is an output statement or alias whose function is additionally an operator of its own. Every statement that contributes an operation named in R7 (an expectation, a maximization, a solution, an authored evaluation or envelope) retains that operation within its expression. A method may address the occurrence without thereby introducing another authored node.

    The stage as a whole has as inputs the continuation value fields and the arrival state, and as outputs the arrival value fields and the continuation state. Its backward operator \(\mathbb T\) carries the continuation value functions to the arrival value functions, and its forward operator \(\mathbb M_\pi\) carries the law of the arrival state to the law of the continuation state.

    Rejection. An output that no statement determines is an error; a policy block whose lines write the candidate at the continuation perch determines its policy only by the line that identifies the control with the candidate (R10). An input that a statement redefines is an error. The package does not yet check this (§5.3). A second block between the same two perches whose output side lists a field that another block writes is an error (one-builder-per-output), and so is a second forward block, or a second block that writes value fields, between the same two perches, even where it writes another field (forward-composite). A coordinate whose set is declared nowhere is an error (§2.4). The package does not yet check this (§5.3). A multiplier is declared only in the body of a policy block. The package does not yet check this (§5.3). A multiplier declaration is also an error when it has no clause, a mark other than [>], a clause of two bounds, or a bound naming a control, a decision or arrival state or a candidate; so are a second declaration of one multiplier and a header that lists a multiplier.

    R2 Ordered statements and substitution¶

    Written form. Several statements in one block.

    Interpretation. Explicit definitions are read in dependency order. An alias is available after its defining statement; an implicit condition is retained as a relation in its designated unknowns (R10), not used as an assignment before those unknowns are determined. A block composes these operations according to their inputs and outputs.

    Substitution. Replacing an explicit alias by its defining expression preserves the field relation wherever the operations are defined. An explicit statement may also be solved for a name that occurs in it once and affinely, with a coefficient that the declared sets show to be nonzero: the result is an equation between the same fields, holding wherever the statement holds, so that the transition a = m_d - c of §4.2 gives m_d = a + c, and a = R*m_d - c with R : ℝ₊₊ gives m_d = (a + c)/R. For a name that occurs more than once, or not affinely, or with a coefficient that the declared sets do not show to be nonzero, as under R : ℝ₊, the statement is not solved; R10 uses this rearrangement to check a coordinate line against the transition, and makes no check where it does not apply. Resolve bindings before substitution (§2.5). A shock dependence reached through an alias or transition belongs inside the expectation containing that use of the alias; a different locally bound variable with the same spelling does not. A !node keeps its statement as an authored node. Dependency analysis and mathematical substitution may still inspect its definition; the mark does not conceal the shocks or controls on which its output depends.

    Assumptions. The composing functions must be defined on the supplied arguments and measurable when a probabilistic interpretation needs them. Grammatical acceptance does not prove measurability, differentiability, invertibility, or existence of a solution.

    R3 Product outputs¶

    Written form. An output side with several coordinates, -> (k[>] : ℝ₊, b[>] : ℝ₊) : !cntn; a tuple expression (e_1, e_2); a selection (e_1, e_2)[d]; a tuple left side (i, b_n) = ….

    Context. The typing of a tuple subject follows the declared form: (w, c) : S declares two fields when S is a product with one factor for each name, written out or named, and each field takes its own factor, so that (w, c) : ℝ₊ × ℝ₊₊ declares w in \(\mathbb R_+\) and c in \(\mathbb R_{++}\). A set that couples the names is refused under readable-form; a constraint that couples a control with a state is written on the binder of the maximization (R7). x : ℝ × ℝ declares one field with values in a product, whose coordinates are reached by selection.

    Interpretation. The output of a block whose side lists several coordinates is the tuple of those fields, so the continuation state space of the example is \(\mathsf X_\succ = \mathbb R_+ \times \mathbb R_+\). Each coordinate is defined by its own statement. A statement with a tuple left side defines several outputs at once only where its right side is one argmax or one solve, as in the joint choice of a policy block (R7, R10); an ordinary tuple assignment, such as (k[>], b[>]) = ((1-δ)*k_d + i, b_n), is refused under not-constructed. The transition \(\mathrm g_\succ : \mathsf X_\sim \times \mathsf C \to \mathsf X_\succ\) has one component per output coordinate. A component may itself be product-valued; coordinate count alone does not determine a Euclidean dimension. A tuple expression denotes a finite family in the written order; a selection (e_1, …, e_J)[d] by a control d valued in a finite set of labels denotes \(e_j\) when \(d\) is the \(j\)-th label in the order the set literal lists them. A collection declared on the functions from a finite integer or label set, δ : L_J → ℝ with L_J := {1, …, J}, denotes at each calibration one function on the members of that set, and δ[j] its value at a key j declared within the index set, never a position; dist[j][i] denotes the entry at the ordered pair, the first key on the first factor. Integer keys identify entries whose values may be real numbers; equal numerical outcomes at two distinct keys do not identify those keys. The forward reading of a product output is the joint law of the tuple (R9), not a product of marginal laws.

    Rejection. A positional selection must be within the family. A selection by a name reads a declared selector variable and never a label written in the brackets, so that (e_1, e_2)[work] is refused even where work is a member of a declared set. A selector variable must have a compatible domain and scope: for a tuple, a finite set with as many members as the tuple has entries, and for a collection on an index set, a set within that index set. A selection outside these forms is refused under family-selection, and on the value line of a branching block under branch-tuple-mismatch. The family and its labels must agree in length and order. A tuple of alternative value functions is distinct from the labeled union of successor state spaces in §4.7.

    R4 Scope, binding, and information¶

    Two questions must be kept apart. Binding asks which occurrences of a name are bound by an operator (§2.5). Determination asks which fields a block takes as given and which it defines (R1). A perch mark answers neither: it asserts at which information set a field is measurable.

    A perch mark is not a binder

    In V = 𝑢(c) + V[>], the continuation value is an input. Its representative is evaluated at the state supplied by the transition. Neither the mark nor the pullback along the law of motion binds the continuation function. In V[<] = E_{y}(V), the shock is integrated after exposing its dependence in the integrand.

    Interpretation. Information checks apply to each operation in its own context, with local bindings and indirect dependencies preserved. An explicit field definition must produce a quantity measurable at its stated perch. On a backward line the construction asks for a function of the state at that perch (§1.2): a state of an earlier perch is refused under forward-composite, and a field of a later perch that is read across an edge that draws a shock is refused under shock-observed unless it stands inside an expectation over each shock drawn on the way. The package does not yet check this for a shock that the header of a backward block lists and that is drawn on an earlier edge or on no edge: the line reads it as known, and the function that the line defines keeps the shock free (§5.3). An implicit equation instead defines a relation to be solved under the information available to its unknowns; it is not a claim that every intermediate expression already has that measurability. Its two sides are one relation (R10), and a term on either side that reads a field of a later perch is read through the law of motion and, across an edge that draws a shock, inside an expectation over that shock. The package does not yet check this on the left side, for a term outside an expectation that combines a field of a later perch, or its derivative, with other quantities, such as β*D_{a}V[>] (§5.3).

    Declared dependencies give a sufficient information bound, not necessarily the smallest information set of the mathematical expression: a shock may cancel algebraically. A construction that does not prove such cancellation may report that the required measurability has not been established, but must not assert that the expression is mathematically nonmeasurable for that reason or insert an expectation. The package takes as declared dependencies the shocks that the forward blocks between the two perches list, whether or not a law of motion reads them, and it refuses under shock-observed a field of the later perch that is read outside an expectation over them. The message of that refusal says that the value is not known at the earlier perch; the package does not yet word it as a report that measurability has not been established (§5.3).

    For example, at arrival E_{y}(V + y) integrates the designated shock in both terms, whereas E_{y}(V) + y retains a free final y. If y is not known at arrival, the latter is not an arrival-field definition. The mere presence of an expectation elsewhere in the equation supplies no exemption.

    Consequence for the two written forms. In the perch form the binders are visible in the expression, E_{y} binding y and nothing else. In the functional form the same information must be carried by the state arguments: the integrand is written as the function evaluated at the state that contains the bound shock, \(\mathrm v_\sim(y + R\,b)\), and the free names \(b\) and \(R\) appear as arguments or parameters (R5, §4.4).

    R5 Fields to functions¶

    This is the central rule: it passes from a statement between fields to a relation between functions of the state, and it records what the passage assumes.

    Written form. A statement Z = F(Y_1, …, Y_n) whose left side is a field at perch \(p\) and whose right side mentions fields \(Y_i\) at perches \(q_i\) and parameters, after the aliases and declared functions of R2 have been expanded, so that F is the function the expression denotes. The package does not yet print the expanded expression on the card of a builder, the record of the builder that denote() prints (A piece §5), where the block writes an alias before the output statement: the rule that the card prints for the output is then the alias's line, while the function that the operation computes is the expanded expression (§5.3).

    Context. The state arguments, the declared transitions, the functions supplied as inputs, and any recovery maps for earlier quantities needed as functions of the chosen state.

    Interpretation. Require:

    • (A1) each input field has a representative on its declared domain, \(Y_i = \mathrm y_i(X_{q_i})\) almost surely. For fields being constructed, establish the required state sufficiency (§1.2).
    • (A2) a later state is supplied by the declared measurable transition \(X_{q_i} = \mathrm t_i(X_p,c,\xi)\), where \(c\) collects retained controls and \(\xi\) the shocks on that transition. At the same state, \(\mathrm t_i\) is the identity.
    • (A3) an earlier quantity used as a function of the current state has an explicit representation in the available arguments, which a line of the file supplies. A field whose name is a coordinate \(x\) of perch \(p\), written with the mark of another perch \(q\), denotes that coordinate read at \(q\), and in a policy block a line x[q] = e supplies the map \(\mathrm r_x\), the function e denotes at \(q\), from the state at \(q\) to the coordinate \(x\) of \(p\). Under the continuation mark, \(x\) is a state of the decision perch, and a line there for a state of another perch is refused under candidate-coordinate. In a value block or an arrival block the line supplies no map: under the arrival mark it is retained as a relation that no operation realizes, which stage.obligations(), the list of the conditions the stage records, holds as retained-relation, and under the continuation mark it is refused under input-perch-state. In the consumption stage of §4.2, m_d[>] = a + c[>] supplies the decision coordinate read at the continuation perch, the coordinate map \(\varphi(a) = a + \mathrm f(a)\) of R8; (A2) supplies the reading in the other direction, a later state read at an earlier perch through the transition. Inclusion of earlier information in \(\mathcal F_p\) does not make a non-injective transition invertible or ensure that the current state retains that quantity.

    With no remaining shock arguments, pointwise composition gives $$ \mathrm z(x,c)=F\bigl(\mathrm y_1(\mathrm t_1(x,c)),\ldots, \mathrm y_n(\mathrm t_n(x,c))\bigr). $$ Here the appropriate argument map for each component includes any recovery map required by (A3). With shocks present, retain them as explicit arguments until the authored operator integrates them using R7. Their appearance alone does not prove that the final expression depends on them: cancellation or degeneracy may remove that dependence (R4).

    The control is retained until a policy is specified. In a deterministic consumption transition, the written value equation gives $$ Q(x,c)=\mathrm u(c)+\mathrm v_\succ(\mathrm g_\succ(x,c)),\qquad \mathrm v_\sim(x)=Q(x,\pi(x)). $$ The second operation evaluates the first at a policy; a discount factor appears only when authored. For a stochastic transition, an expectation must occur at its written position, not be inserted by substitution.

    Evaluation must be specified

    E_{η}(V[>]) integrates a field whose representative is evaluated at \(\mathrm g_\succ(x,c,\eta)\). Thus the integrand is \(\mathrm v_\succ(\mathrm g_\succ(x,c,\eta))\). An operator on functions could be defined separately, but these rules do not interpret an unqualified \(\mathbb E_\eta\,\mathrm v_\succ\) as that evaluation.

    Implicit dependence. If a substituted argument depends on the output being determined, the substitution produces a residual in that unknown. It does not fail; it gives the implicit relation of R10. The package does not yet read the statement in this way where its left side is the bare control and the dependence enters through an alias (§5.3). An alias such as z = c[>] can describe dependence on an undetermined control without itself supplying an equation that determines that control.

    A statement across two perches. A statement Y = Z whose left side is at perch \(p\) and whose right side is at perch \(q\) denotes one relation, and the relation may be rewritten at either perch by putting for the other side's state its representation there, the transition of (A2) or the map of (A3). Rewritten at \(q\) it reads \(\mathrm y \circ \mathrm r = \mathrm z\) on the state at \(q\); rewritten at \(p\) it reads \(\mathrm y = \mathrm z \circ \mathrm g\), which contains the unknown when Z depends on it and is then the implicit form of R10. Where the map and the transition are consistent (R10), the two rewritings denote the same set of pairs, each substitution reversing the other. No unknown is assigned, so R2's rule that an implicit condition is retained as a relation stands; the construction rewrites the relation at the perch whose map contains no unknown. The package does not yet do this for every such statement (§5.3).

    Where the equality holds. An almost-sure field equality determines representatives only almost everywhere under the relevant state law. A written formula or supplied continuation function may instead specify a pointwise function on a declared domain. Use those domain-defined functions for policy comparisons, new initial distributions, and transition substitution. Almost-everywhere equality under one distribution alone does not justify substitution at states to which a different policy assigns positive probability. Retain the representative/domain choices or establish that the changed law does not give positive probability to the exceptional set. Neither diagram silently upgrades an almost-sure identity to a pointwise one.

    R6 Well-formedness¶

    Five checks apply to every statement. The first two check the declarations: the first records the conditions that the declared sets leave open, and the second finds contradictions between roles. The third and fourth check whether the supplied context justifies the claimed field interpretation. The fifth finds blocks that determine less than they promise.

    1. Domain. Every name has a set (§2.4). The package does not yet check this for a field or a shock that a block header lists (§5.3). A function applied to an argument whose set is not known to lie in the declared set of its formal argument is not refused: the stage records the condition function-argument-domain-inclusion, which stage.obligations() lists as required and calibration does not decide. 𝑢(c) with 𝑢(c : ℝ₊₊) and c : ℝ₊ records one, and the file removes it by declaring the control in \(\mathbb R_{++}\). A restriction that the declared sets do not state is recorded as a condition as well, and calibration decides it where it reads parameters alone: the body c^(1-γ)/(1-γ) under γ : ℝ₊ records that \(1 - \gamma\) is not zero, which a calibration at \(\gamma = 2\) establishes, and a calibration at \(\gamma = 1\) is refused. A declared set can state such a restriction: under γ : ℝ₊₊ \ {1} a calibration at \(\gamma = 1\) is refused as a value outside the declared set. A restriction that reads a state, a control or the argument of a function stays required and is not checked.
    2. Role. A name has at most one role. A control is at the decision perch. A value field carries !vlu; the derivative of a value is read by a derivative term and has no role. A shock has a law or the word !shck.
    3. Information. Apply the local rules of R4 and R7 to explicit definitions. An unresolved shock or a missing current-state representation requires additional context, an authored operation, or a diagnostic. Algebraic cancellation can justify more cases than a conservative dependency check establishes.
    4. Scope. Resolve every reference to a declaration, a local definition, or a binder. A bare name that resolves to none of them is refused under name-in-scope. A call of a function that the file does not declare, such as log(c) or the misspelling 𝑢𝑢(c) beside a declared 𝑢, is not refused: it is read as a function supplied from outside the file, the stage records the conditions external-callable-signature-and-provider and external-callable-result-domain, and the stage's diagnostics, stage.diagnostics(), list the call under external_calls. On a backward line the package does not yet resolve the bare name of a decision state where a state at another perch has the same name, and it refuses the line under name-in-scope (§1.1, §5.3). A field that a forward block reads is listed in its header or defined earlier in the block. So is a value field or a control that a backward block reads, and a shock that it reads outside an expectation over that shock. A backward block lists no state: it reads a state of the perch of its output without a listing, and a state of its input perch only through the law of motion (R1). Using a field still requires the appropriate state representation. For example, knowledge of decision w does not make w[>] = w a function of the saving state a without a recovery relation. Check references and selections inside functions and distribution declarations too.
    5. Boundary. Each required output has an explicit definition or a justified relation in its unknowns. Several coupled conditions must be treated as a simultaneous system, not competing assignments. A declared output port or an empty body supplies no defining relation.

    Inconsistency and assumption. Distinguish an inconsistent declaration from a missing rule or premise; do not call an unproved analytic property a contradiction. Sufficiency (A1), integrability (R7), existence and measurable selection of maximizers and roots (R8, R10), uniqueness where required, and concavity where a first-order condition is used are assumptions; the rules record them beside the operator that needs them and do not verify them.

    R7 The operations a line writes¶

    A block's operator is composed from five operations that its lines write, each with its own mathematical requirement. The primitive operators of the block's builder hold them; a maximization written on a line not marked !doc, for instance, stands in an operation of the kind Argmax, and the residuals of a solution in an Aggregation (R8, R10).

    Application. Arithmetic and the application of a function, 𝑢(c), log(c), act pointwise and preserve the perch of their arguments. A function that the file does not declare, such as log, is read as a function supplied from outside the file, under the conditions that R6 names. D_{c}(𝑢) denotes the derivative of the declared function with respect to its named argument and assumes differentiability. The package does not yet check that the subscript names an argument of the function (§2.1, §5.3). It does not yet record the assumption of differentiability as a condition of the stage (§5.3). Inv(𝑑𝑢) denotes the functional inverse and assumes that the function is a bijection between the declared set of its argument and its range; for the marginal utility of power utility with strictly positive risk aversion, \(\mathbb R_{++}\) onto \(\mathbb R_{++}\). The stage records this assumption as the condition inverse-function-domain.

    Expectation. Interpret E_{ζ}(e) in its current conditioning context \(\mathcal G\). For an outermost arrival-value equation this is \(\mathcal F_\prec\); nested integrations must retain their own still-free arguments and conditional laws. Suppose \(e=F(X,\zeta)\), with \(X\) \(\mathcal G\)-measurable, and the specified kernel \(K_\zeta\) satisfies \(\mathbb P(\zeta\in A\mid\mathcal G)=K_\zeta(X,A)\) almost surely for measurable sets \(A\). A probability kernel assigns a distribution to each conditioning argument and depends measurably on that argument. Then $$ \mathbb E[F(X,\zeta)\mid\mathcal G] =\int F(X,z)\,K_\zeta(X,\mathrm dz)\quad\text{almost surely}, $$ for a measurable integrable \(F(X,\zeta)\) (Kallenberg 1997, Theorem 5.4). The specified kernel and its dependence on \(X\) are necessary premises. Standard Borel shock spaces support regular conditional distributions; the chosen state must still suffice to parameterize the required law.

    The integral binds \(z\). Other free quantities must be part of the supplied conditioning context or remain explicit dependencies of an enclosing operation. They are not automatically independent or deterministic. E_{Ψ,θ} uses the joint conditional law; successive integrations use its conditional factorization, in which separately declared parent-free shocks are conditionally independent given the source coordinates. The same left-side perch cannot be assigned to every inner expectation without checking those contexts.

    The elaboration selects that conditional law at each occurrence. Let \(R\) be the shocks the integrand still depends on, \(H\) the shocks earlier occurrences on its paths have integrated, \(S\) the shocks the occurrence names, and \(A(S)\) the shock arguments and other subject components of the declarations of \(S\). The occurrence conditions on \(C = ((R \setminus S) \cup A(S)) \setminus (S \cup H)\) and the source coordinates, and uses the law of \(S\) given \(C\) derived from the joint law of the edge whose law of motion the block substitutes: a declared conditional, a product of declared conditionals in dependency order, or a marginal or conditional of a joint normal declaration whose conditioning covariance block is positive definite. Every shock of \(S\) is drawn on that edge: an expectation over a shock that another edge draws, or that no edge draws, is refused under distribution-edge. The result of the occurrence is a function of the source coordinates and \(C\), and the shocks of \(S\) outside \(C\) join \(H\). At a pointwise combination, remove from the combined history the shocks on which any genuine sibling value input still depends. A later explicit arithmetic read is refused only if its shock remains in this history (shock-reintroduced). Any other conditional, a posterior among them, is refused (conditional-not-declared). Under ν ~ N(κ_1, σ_ν^2) and Ψ ~ LogNormal(ν, σ_Ψ) the order E_{ν}(E_{Ψ}(·)) uses the law of \(\Psi\) given \(\nu\) and then the marginal of \(\nu\); the reversed order would need the law of \(\nu\) given \(\Psi\) and is refused. In the objective of a policy block the package does not yet refuse the reversed order under this rule: it refuses it under shock-observed (§5.3). A block output depends on no shock, and every shock of the edge is integrated on each path to it (shock-observed).

    For finite real-valued results require the relevant integrals to be finite. Nonnegative or one-sided-integrable expressions can also have extended-real conditional expectations, provided that extended values belong to the output domain and subsequent arithmetic does not form undefined expressions such as \(+\infty-\infty\). A sum with a term \(-\infty\) and no term \(+\infty\) is \(-\infty\), the value of an infeasible point. These are analytic requirements, not consequences of the operator's syntax.

    Maximization and maximum. With feasible controls \(\mathcal C(x)\) and choice value \(Q(x,c)\), argmax_{c}(e) names at each state \(x\) the maximizer set of the display below, and the line that writes it denotes the set of maximizing policies (R8). A policy, where one is required, is a measurable selection from the maximizer set, $$ \pi(x)\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{c\in\mathcal C(x)}Q(x,c). $$ The binders stand, in order, for the fields that the left side of the line writes (§2.5). A line whose binders and left-side fields differ in number is refused, under well-typed, or under output-defined where an output of the block is thereby left without a definition. A feasibility correspondence can specify a product of sets or a product restricted by conditions, as Constraints §1 shows. A call Γ(x) substitutes x for the definition's formal argument in every factor and condition, while the member named by a comprehension remains bound. Thus a triple constrained by q[1] + q[2] = z becomes a triple constrained by q[1] + q[2] = x at that call. Its result values are still triples, with the equality retained as a restriction on the whole product; neither the formal argument z nor the bound member q becomes an additional free state coordinate.

    The operation of the kind Argmax that a line not marked !doc forms has as its output the set of maximizing policies, the measurable policies that select a member of the maximizer set at every state; the set may be empty or hold several policies. The closing Evaluation of the policy builder selects no member of it, and the selection is the numerical method's. The maximum value \(\max_{c}Q(x,c)\) may be well-defined without a unique maximizer or a chosen policy. Nonempty compact feasible sets and upper semicontinuity in the control ensure attainment; measurability of a selection requires additional conditions on the state dependence.

    Technical details

    One sufficient measurable-maximum theorem uses a separable metric control space, a weakly measurable feasible correspondence with nonempty compact values, and an objective measurable in the state and continuous in the control. Weak measurability means that the states whose feasible set meets each open control set form a measurable set. Under these hypotheses the maximizer correspondence has a measurable selection (Aliprantis and Border 2006, Theorem 18.19). Pointwise attainment alone is insufficient.

    Where several maximizers exist, keep the selection requirement explicit; the syntax supplies no automatic tie-break.

    Solution. solve_{c}(r_1, …, r_k) denotes the relation of feasible unknowns satisfying every residual equation. The package does not yet form this relation for one unknown with two or more residuals: it reads such a line without a refusal, and the rule of its operation holds no residual (§5.3). A unique solution gives a single-valued map; several solutions require a specified selection to produce one. In either case measurability is an additional requirement. Existence, selection, and optimality are separate questions (R8, R10).

    Evaluation. Evaluation of a given function at a point is ordinary application. Finite pairs (knots, values) determine a function only on their listed coordinates, with repeated coordinates permitted only when the values agree. They do not uniquely determine a function between knots. The form evaluate(knots, values, x) therefore requires a stated numerical representation, including interpolation and any extrapolation, before it has a numerical value off the knots. For exact candidate/coordinate maps, evaluation instead uses the function determined by their relation under R10. Keep this exact interpretation distinct from its tabulated approximation.

    Levels of detail. When the required conditions determine a block's operator, its statements describe the constituents of that operator, and the occurrences of the operations above inside the statements are the finest constituents. A numerical method may be attached to the block, to a declared function, to a statement marked !node or a line that has an id, or to an occurrence; the choice of level is the methods file's, and it does not change what the block denotes. Every mathematical operation remains present at its appropriate detail, including within an unmarked equation. Only !node creates a separately authored node; a method's convenience does not create a new authored equation or place.

    The result of an occurrence. An occurrence of an operation inside an unmarked equation, E_{η}(V[>]) in the line V = 𝑢(c) + E_{η}(V[>]) of a backward block whose header lists the control c, receives a function of the arguments the equation has bound so far and returns a function of the arguments that remain. The block's builder is the operator at a policy (R1), so the integrand is \(\mathrm v_\succ(\mathrm g_\succ(x, \pi(x), \eta))\) and the result is a function of the decision state \(x\) alone; the same occurrence in the objective of the policy block, c = argmax_{c}(𝑢(c) + E_{η}(V[>])), returns a function of (x, c). The statement's aggregation reads that result as its value input, and whether it is affine in that input is a property of the statement, never a condition of construction. That result exists in the composed operator, because the occurrence is one of the operations above and has its own domain condition; it is not an authored object. Only the operation's own methods attach to it (a quadrature rule to an expectation, a maximizer to a maximization, a root finder to a solution), and nothing may represent it: its value is obtained exactly, through its definition, wherever the equation reads it. A representation, an interpolant or a table on a grid, attaches only to a declared function, a builder output, a statement marked !node, a line that has an id, where it represents the function that the line defines, or an authored evaluate, where the representation is the method by which that evaluation is computed; an author who wants the expectation held as an object writes it on its own line and gives the line an id or marks it !node. The two questions are therefore separate: whether a function exists as an object is decided by the source (the block outputs, the !node marks and the ids of lines), and whether an existing object is exact or an approximation is decided by the methods file, with exact as the default. Attaching a method to an occurrence, a quadrature rule to E_{η}(·), does not make the occurrence a node: a sub-builder is created by !node alone, whether or not the marked line cuts off an expectation, and a method's attachment changes neither the graph nor what the occurrence is.

    R8 Evaluation and policy¶

    Three operations are easily confused and must be kept distinct.

    Evaluation at a policy. Given the choice value \(Q(x, c)\) and a policy \(\pi\), the decision value function is \(\mathrm v_\sim(x) = Q(x, \pi(x))\). No optimality is involved; this is the policy operator \(T_\pi\) of Sargent and Stachurski, and it is what a stage computes when the policy is given from outside.

    Maximization. A line c = argmax_{c}(…) denotes the set of maximizing policies: the measurable policies \(\pi\) with \(\pi(x) \in \arg\max_{c \in \mathcal C(x)} Q(x, c)\) at every state \(x\), which are the measurable selections from the maximizer set of R7. The set may be empty or hold several policies. Under the assumptions of R7 it holds a policy, and evaluation at a policy in it gives \(\mathrm v_\sim(x) = \max_{c} Q(x, c)\). The two steps together are the maximum; the calculus writes them as evaluation composed with selection (Operators and builders §7). The stage states the set, and the selection is the numerical method's (R7).

    Solution of a first-order condition. A solve statement or an implicit statement (R10) determines \(\pi(x)\) as a root of a residual \(r(x, c) = 0\). Existence and an appropriate measurable root selection are required. Uniqueness is one sufficient way to determine that selection. The root yields an optimizer only with additional conditions, such as a differentiable concave objective on a convex feasible domain and the appropriate first-order conditions; strict concavity supplies uniqueness when a maximizer exists. An equality residual states the interior condition; where the control's bound can bind, the file must state the corner condition, by the clause ⟂ of R10 on the first-order condition, by the clause on the continuation-marked candidate line, c[>] <= 𝑑𝑢𝑖𝑛𝑣(β*D_{a}V[>]) ⟂ a >= 0 (§4.5), by a multiplier declared in the policy block with the clause on its declaration (R10), or by a separately declared branch, or the statement determines the policy only on the region where the interior condition has a solution.

    The endogenous-grid policy. In a policy block whose header names the continuation value V[>] and whose lines read its derivative by the term D_{a}V[>], the continuation-marked candidate lines read the continuation state a as their coordinate. Such a line writes a control under the continuation mark, c[>], a state of the decision perch under the mark, w[>], or the value candidate that an upper_env of the block compares (R12); a line that writes another name under the mark is refused, as z[>] = 2*c[>] is under candidate-coordinate. The statements c[>] = 𝑑𝑢𝑖𝑛𝑣(β*D_{a}V[>]) and w[>] = a + c[>] define two fields at the continuation perch, represented with respect to \(a\) by the candidate \(\mathrm f(a) = \mathrm{du}^{-1}(\beta\,\mathrm{dv}_\succ(a))\) and the coordinate map \(\varphi(a) = a + \mathrm f(a)\). The final line c = c[>] states the identification of the control with the candidate. Read at the decision perch it is an implicit equation in \(c\) at the saving \(a = w - c\) (R10); rewritten at the continuation perch through the coordinate map (R5), it states \(\pi(\varphi(a)) = \mathrm f(a)\), the control at the cash on hand \(w = \varphi(a)\) equal to the candidate there. Under R10's consistency and single-valuedness conditions the two readings give the same policy on the represented decision domain. A block that omits the final line determines no policy and is refused (R1). A line that writes a candidate which no later line of the block reads is refused under candidate-read; the coordinate line, w[>] = a + c[>], needs no reader. If \(\varphi\) is injective, this policy can be written \(\pi = \mathrm f \circ \varphi^{-1}\); injectivity is sufficient but is not the general condition. The tabulation of \((\varphi(a_i), \mathrm f(a_i))\) on an asset grid and the interpolation between the points are the numerical method's approximation of this composition; they are not operations of the stage. An authored c = evaluate(w[>], c[>], w) expresses evaluation of the determined relation where such a functional interpretation is supplied. With finite tables, it instead evaluates the chosen approximation (R7). The package does not yet construct either of these readings: it reads the line and records its rule without a definition of evaluate, also where the methods file types a representation at the line (§5.3).

    Marginal values. A marginal value is the derivative of a value and no field: a line reads it by the derivative term, D_{a}V[>] for the derivative of the continuation value in the continuation coordinate a, which denotes \(\partial_a \mathrm v_\succ\), written \(\mathrm{dv}_\succ\) on this page, a function of \(\mathrm v_\succ\) and not a second object. The value functions are read on the domain of locally Lipschitz functions on the declared set, on which the one-sided derivative exists everywhere and the derivative is single-valued almost everywhere and set-valued at a kink, as a value with a discrete choice downstream is at its switching points; differentiability at the point a line reads is a condition the rules record, not a type. The package does not yet read a derivative in this way: it defines the derivative as the limit of the difference quotients, a single number, and it records no condition at a line that reads a derivative (§5.3). stage.obligations() lists differentiability only for a derivative line, under marginal-reading, and for a policy builder under the endogenous grid method, under egm-eligibility. A derivative line (§2.2), D_{w}V = 𝑑𝑢(c) in the value builder's block of the stage file, or the same line in the block of the methods file that names the builder's block by its id, [id=cntn_to_dcsn], states the derivative of the value the builder writes, in the state coordinate of the perch at which that value stands. It is the author's assertion about that value, an identity that holds under the envelope conditions, and not the definition of a new field; it proves nothing. Under an envelope theorem's hypotheses, the derivative of an optimized value is obtained from the objective's state derivative, with any state-dependent constraints treated correctly (Benveniste and Scheinkman 1979). Differentiability and interiority alone are not a general formula for that derivative.

    For \(Q(w,c)=\mathrm u(c)+\beta\,\mathrm v_\succ(w-c)\), the interior Euler condition and a valid envelope theorem give \(\mathrm v_\sim'(w)=\beta\,\mathrm v_\succ'(w-\pi(w))= \mathrm u'(\pi(w))\). This justifies the example's D_{w}V = 𝑑𝑢(c) under these conditions: concavity of the continuation value; interiority of the maximizer or, at a binding constraint, the multiplier's term as the Kuhn–Tucker clause states it; differentiability of the reward in the control; uniqueness or a measurable selection of the policy; and differentiability of the value at the point read (Milgrom and Segal 2002; Clausen and Strub 2020). Other rewards, transition derivatives, or binding constraints need their own derivative line. No term and no role supplies a formula that is absent from the written lines: nothing is differentiated, and the only analytic source of a derivative is a line the author writes.

    The lines of a solution method on the policy builder. A stage file may write its policy block with the maximization alone and leave the computation to its methods file, whose block policy types a solution method on the policy builder and brings the method's lines (Methodization §5). Those lines are read by these rules as the lines of a stage block are, with the stage's declarations in scope: for the endogenous grid method (Carroll 2006), the candidate lines of the endogenous-grid paragraph above, the candidate line carrying the clause of R10. Their operations are the rows of the operative policy builder, stage.builder; the declared builder, stage.declared, and the initial diagram keep the maximization, which the builder's card prints under Declared as the line whose policy the rows are to compute. The rows reach it only under a condition that the stage records and nothing checks, listed by stage.obligations() under the method table's name for it: for the endogenous grid method, egm-eligibility, that the continuation value \(\mathrm v_\succ\) is concave and continuously differentiable on \((0,\infty)\) with a finite positive right derivative at zero. Under it the candidate \(\mathrm f\) is continuous and nondecreasing, the coordinate map \(\varphi\) is strictly increasing from \(\mathrm f(0)\) to infinity, and the policy is \(\mathrm f \circ \varphi^{-1}\) at cash on hand of at least \(\mathrm f(0)\) and the whole of cash on hand below it (§4.5). The package does not yet record such a condition for another solution method: under !time_iteration or !explicit the method's lines stand in place of the maximization, and stage.obligations() lists no condition under which they compute its policy (§5.3).

    Derivative lines of the methods file. Where the stage file writes no derivative line, the author writes it in the methods file, in a block that names the backward value builder's block by its id and carries no signature. The block's lines are derivative lines of the value that builder writes, D_{w}V = 𝑑𝑢(c). Each line is recorded at the builder's Evaluation as a stated derivative, beside the method that represents the value; it adds no wire and no box, and the builder's signature is unchanged, \((\mathrm V_\succ) \mapsto (\mathrm V)\), whether the line is added or not. Nothing is differentiated: each line is a line the author wrote, and its reading as the derivative of the value the builder writes is the author's assertion, which stage.obligations() lists as marginal-reading and nothing checks. How the derivative of a value is evaluated is a property of the value, fixed where the value is built: by the stated line, whose values the representation at the builder's Evaluation stores, or by the representation of the value itself; the methods file types that representation at the Evaluation, cntn_to_dcsn.evaluate for the value and its stated derivative together, or cntn_to_dcsn.evaluate[D_{m}V] for the derivative alone. A block that reads D_{a}V[>] declares that reading and nothing else; it knows only that the value it reads is a function. At a constrained choice the line is read at the policy, where the multiplier of the constraint is the residual of the first-order condition. For \(Q(w,c)=\mathrm u(c)+\beta\,\mathrm v_\succ(w-c)\) with the bound \(w - c \ge 0\) and its multiplier \(\mu^*\), \(\mathrm v_\sim'(w)=\beta\,\mathrm v_\succ'(a^*)+\mu^*=\mathrm u'(\pi(w))\) at the optimal saving \(a^* = w-\pi(w)\), the corner included through the right derivative at zero saving; the clause of the third spelling states this multiplier, and the declared multiplier of the second spelling is read the same way (Constraints §4). For a parametrized convex program the multipliers are the subgradients of the optimal value as a function of the constraint's perturbation (Rockafellar 1970, Theorem 29.1), and they exist under Slater's condition (Theorem 28.2). In the dynamic setting the derivative of the value function is given by the envelope theorems of Benveniste and Scheinkman (1979), as Stokey, Lucas and Prescott (1989, Theorems 4.10 and 4.11) state them for an interior policy, of Milgrom and Segal (2002) for arbitrary choice sets, and of Rincón-Zapatero and Santos (2009) without interiority assumptions.

    When the split is not exact. Several controls solved jointly are one solve with several unknowns (R10). An occasionally binding constraint is the clause ⟂, a declared multiplier (R10) or a branch (R3, §4.7). A policy that feeds back into the transition or the feasible set through an aggregate, as in an equilibrium model, is a fixed point above the stage and not an object of these rules.

    R9 The forward reading¶

    Written form. The same transition blocks and law statements as the backward reading, together with the policy the policy block determines. A statement of a transition block defines the law of motion and contains no expectation. A statement there that integrates a shock, as w = E_{y}(y) + b*R would in the arrival transition of cons_savings_iid (§4.2), is refused under transition-without-expectation; an integration over a shock is written in a backward block or in the policy block (R7, R11).

    Interpretation. Under a measurable feasible policy \(\pi\), the declared transitions and conditional shock laws determine a state-transition kernel. For either edge write it as \(K(x,A)\): the probability that the next state belongs to a measurable set \(A\), conditional on the current state \(x\). Its forward action on a state law \(\mu\) and backward action on a test function \(v\) are $$ (K_#\mu)(A)=\int K(x,A)\,\mu(\mathrm dx),\qquad (K^v)(x)=\int v(y)\,K(x,\mathrm dy). $$ For bounded measurable \(v\), or the corresponding integrable cases, $$ \int v\,\mathrm d(K_#\mu)=\int K^v\,\mathrm d\mu. $$ This is the forward/backward pairing identity. Composition of transitions propagates measures forward and functions in reverse order. It does not make the full Bellman operator the transpose of a probability transition: with reward and constant discount, \(T_\pi v=r_\pi+\beta K_\pi^*v\) is generally affine, and maximization over policies is generally nonlinear. The pairing concerns \(K_\pi^*\); rewards, discounting, and optimization remain their separately authored operations.

    For a stage the forward composition is \(\mathbb M_\pi=\mathbb M_{\succ,\pi}\circ\mathbb M_\prec\). A product output has the joint law of its components, and a selected branch has its law on the appropriate labeled successor space. For a branching block the stage records the condition mass-conservation, that the masses of the branch measures sum to the mass of the measure pushed forward. The condition is established where a control selects the branch, or a shock selects it with weights written as numbers, provided that the stage establishes that every line of a branch writes a value in the declared set of its field; otherwise stage.obligations() lists it as required. State-space sufficiency must include the conditional kernels used in this composition.

    Dependence. A joint conditional law, or the declared conditional laws that factor it, specifies shocks used together. Separate declarations define the product of the declared conditional laws, each given its named parents and the source coordinates, and the forward extension adds them in that dependency order, parents first. Declarations that have no such order are refused under distribution-dependency: a declaration that reads a component of its own subject as an argument, (y, z) ~ N((y, 0), …), and declarations whose shock arguments form a cycle, y ~ N(z, 1) with z ~ N(y, 1). The factorization is an assumption the elaboration records; it is not inferred from distinct variable names.

    Scope. Forward propagation needs the transitions, laws, and a supplied policy. Value equations are needed if that policy is itself obtained by solving the stage, but are not needed to propagate a given policy. Recording additional quantities along individual histories is a separate simulation choice.

    R10 Implicit equations¶

    Written form. A statement whose right side depends on its own left side through a transition; a statement whose left side is an expression, 𝑑𝑢(c) = β*D_{a}V[>]; a solve statement; a decision-perch statement followed by the clause ⟂ m_d - c >= 0 where the decision-to-continuation law is a = m_d - c; a continuation-marked candidate line with ⟂ a >= 0; an equation followed by a clause with two bounds, ⟂ 0 <= c_p <= w_d; and a multiplier declared in the body of a policy block, μ[>] : ℝ₊ ⟂ a >= 0.

    Context. The block's outputs, and the transitions through which a right side may reach the output being determined.

    Interpretation. The unknown of an implicit statement is the output of the block that the statement determines, and a bare control on the left side names it. Where the left side of an equation is neither a name, nor a tuple of names, nor one derivative term, the statement is a candidate statement, and its candidates are the outputs of its block that it mentions, bare or under a perch mark, and that no operative statement of the block with a name on its left side defines. A line whose left side is one derivative term, D_{a}V[>], is no candidate statement: every block reads it as a derivative line (§2.2). In a policy block D_{a}V[>] = 𝑑𝑢(c) ⟂ m_d - c >= 0 is therefore refused, under forward-composite, and the first-order condition is written with the term on its right side, 𝑑𝑢(c) = D_{a}V[>] ⟂ m_d - c >= 0. A statement is operative when the block computes with it, which is every line that the author neither marks !doc nor attaches to another line as its property by a dotted id (§2.3); a line marked !doc states what an object is and no operation realizes it, so the maximization statement of a policy block leaves the control it names undetermined here, and a property line forms no operation, defines no output and enters no system. A candidate statement mentions a field when the field's name occurs on either side of its equation after the substitution of aliases (R2): an argument of a call is an occurrence, the body of a called function is not read, no transition is followed, the clause after ⟂ is not read, and a name under a perch mark, c[>], is a field of its own and no occurrence of the bare c. The candidate statements of one block that share a candidate form one system, and one that shares none is a system of one; the unknowns of a system are its candidates in the order of the block's header, a marked field directly after the output it marks. A system with as many statements as unknowns determines them jointly, as a solve statement over several unknowns does (R8), with the residuals in file order and the complementarity clauses of this rule retained. A system whose statements and unknowns differ in number, both being positive, determines nothing and is refused; the relation is not retained as a correspondence. A candidate statement with no candidate determines nothing the block must supply and is retained as a relation the block realizes by no operation, as is an inequality whose left side is not a name. The statement denotes the relation \(\{(x, c) : r(x, c) = 0\}\), with \(r\) the residual, left side minus right side, after the substitution of R5. The output is the field \(c\) with \(r(X_\sim, c) = 0\) almost surely. The entire relation is the graph of a function where there is exactly one solution; a selected part may define a function even when the relation has several solutions. Existence and measurability of that selection, and whether its root is an optimizer, remain separate requirements (R8). A fresh left-hand name does not make a statement explicit: z = c[>], where c[>] depends on the undetermined output c through a = w - c, defines z as a function of c and leaves c undetermined.

    The operation of a system. A system forms one operation of the policy builder, an Aggregation whose rule holds the relation with the system's unknowns on both sides, reached by one solve occurrence at its first statement that holds the system's unknowns, so that a method attached to the policy block addresses the system as one place. The package does not yet record this occurrence on every system (§5.3). On the first-order condition 𝑑𝑢(c) = D_{a}V[>] ⟂ m_d - c >= 0, the example of this paragraph, no place policy.solve exists, a root finder has no place at which to be typed, and a solution method is typed at the policy builder, as policy.method : !time_iteration is. The rule of the operation is the conjunction, over the system's statements, of each statement read at the decision state \(x\): every function at the coordinates of its own perch, every continuation coordinate replaced by the matching component of the law of motion at \(x\) and the policy, \(\mathrm g_\succ(x, \pi(x))\), and a clause read in the same way and held whole beside its statement. The package does not yet read the left side of a statement in this way: a continuation value written there is not composed with the law of motion (R4, §5.3). The rule holds at every \(x\) in the declared decision domain. For the consumption stage of §4.2 with its three policy lines replaced by 𝑑𝑢(c) = D_{a}V[>] ⟂ m_d - c >= 0 it is $$ \mathrm{du}(\pi(x)) = \mathrm{dv}\succ(\mathrm g\succ(x, \pi(x))) \ \perp\ \mathrm g_\succ(x, \pi(x)) \ge 0 \qquad\text{for every } x \in X_m , $$ the Kuhn–Tucker system of the next paragraph at every state. The package does not yet record this rule. The rule that it records takes the derivative, in the continuation coordinate, of the continuation value already composed with the law of motion. That composed function does not depend on the continuation coordinate, so the derivative that the recorded rule states is zero (§1.5, §5.3).

    Complementarity. A clause pairs the residual \(r\), left side minus right side, with the slack \(s\) of its bound on a quantity \(z\): \(s = z - b\) for z >= b, written \(z\) when \(b\) is zero, and \(s = b - z\) for z <= b. A bound is written with >= or <=. The package does not yet check this for a clause with one bound: it reads a strict sign there as the weak one, so that ⟂ a > 0 states the condition of ⟂ a >= 0 (§5.3). In a clause with two bounds a strict sign is refused under readable-form. A lower bound means $$ r\geq0,\qquad s\geq0,\qquad r\,s=0, $$ an upper bound means \(r\leq0\), \(s\geq0\) and \(r\,s=0\), and two bounds, ⟂ lb <= z <= ub, mean $$ z-lb\geq0,\qquad ub-z\geq0,\qquad r\,(z-lb)\leq0,\qquad r\,(ub-z)\geq0 : $$ the residual is nonnegative where the lower bound binds, zero where neither binds and nonpositive where the upper bound binds. After an inequality the residual takes the sign of that relation, the clause adding \(s\geq0\) and \(r\,s=0\); a clause with two bounds after an inequality has no sign rule and is refused. The construction reads a clause by its product: either \(s>0\) and \(r=0\), or \(s=0\) and \(r\) has the stated sign; two bounds give three cases, \(z=lb\) with \(r\geq0\), \(z=ub\) with \(r\leq0\), and both slacks positive with \(r=0\), each case keeping both bounds. The reading admits exactly the pairs the conditions above admit, a strict inequality admitting none where the bound is slack. On the candidate line of §4.5, c[>] <= 𝑑𝑢𝑖𝑛𝑣(β*D_{a}V[>]) ⟂ a >= 0, it admits at each positive saving the one value \(\mathrm f(a)\) and at zero saving every value in \((0, \mathrm f(0)]\). The sign is read from the relation as written, so a clause with one bound writes the bounded quantity first: ⟂ 0 <= a would state the reversed sign and is refused. The clause imposes equality where the bound is slack and a residual sign where the bound binds. Exchanging an equation's sides negates the residual and hence changes this condition. The sign must agree with the constrained problem's first-order conditions. For the Euler residual in §4.5 it gives the stated borrowing-constraint conditions; it does not establish their sufficiency, existence, or domain coverage for an arbitrary model.

    The residual as a multiplier. With one bound the residual is the multiplier of that bound; with two bounds, \(lb < ub\), it is the difference \(\mu_{lo} - \mu_{hi}\) of the two bounds' multipliers, of which at most one is positive. In a system each statement with a clause is written so that its residual is the multiplier of its own bound, the multipliers of the other bounds being eliminated by combining the first-order conditions. In the worker stage of the pension application the second condition is the stationarity condition in the pension deposit less that in consumption, and carries the deposit's two bounds, ⟂ 0 <= c_p <= w_d; in the adjuster stage of the housing application the housing condition carries no clause, because the multiplier of the liquid-asset bound is eliminated from it. The rules hold the statements as written and check no such arrangement; under the rank condition of Dobrescu and Shanker (2024, Assumption 3.3), which makes the multipliers of the binding constraints recoverable from the first-order conditions (their §4.2), one exists.

    The relation of the identification. Let \(\mathrm U:\mathsf S\to\mathsf C\) be the candidate map and \(\mathrm H:\mathsf S\to\mathsf X_\sim\) the coordinate map, the functions that the candidate line and the coordinate line of a policy block denote on the continuation coordinates \(\mathsf S\), the second supplied under R5 (A3). R8 states which names a continuation-marked line may write (candidate-coordinate) and that a later line reads each candidate (candidate-read). Other retained state arguments must be explicit if the displayed map gives only one coordinate. Rewritten at the continuation perch (R5), the identification states \(\pi(\mathrm H(s)) = \mathrm U(s)\) for every \(s \in \mathsf S\), the relation $$ P={(\mathrm H(s),\mathrm U(s)):s\in\mathsf S}. $$ Where the candidate line admits several values at one point, as the clause on the candidate line does at zero saving (§4.5), the pairs range over the admitted values, the coordinate line read at each: \(P=\{(\mathrm H(s, x), x) : s\in\mathsf S,\ x \in \mathrm U(s)\}\). The relation is the identification rewritten, not a rule of its own. A fiber \(\mathrm H^{-1}(x)\) is the set of arguments mapped to \(x\). The relation defines one control at every represented \(x\) exactly when $$ \mathrm H(s_1)=\mathrm H(s_2)\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathrm U(s_1)=\mathrm U(s_2). \tag{F} $$ Indeed, this condition makes \(\pi(\mathrm H(s))=\mathrm U(s)\) independent of the chosen argument; conversely, any such function \(\pi\) makes the two controls equal. Injectivity of \(\mathrm H\) is stronger and gives the convenient formula \(\pi=\mathrm U\circ\mathrm H^{-1}\). For example, \(\mathrm H(s)=s^2\) and \(\mathrm U(s)=s^2\) on \(\mathbb R\) satisfy (F) with \(\pi(x)=x\) on \(\mathbb R_+\) although \(\mathrm H\) is not injective. If \(\mathrm U(s)=s\) instead, the same coordinate map gives two controls at each \(x>0\) and needs a selection rule.

    Single-valuedness is separate from measurability and coverage. Require the induced policy to be measurable on its stated decision domain, and state which boundary or other rule extends it outside \(\mathrm H(\mathsf S)\). A useful sufficient case is a measurable bijection from a standard Borel candidate space onto a declared standard Borel decision domain: its inverse is measurable (Kallenberg 1997, Theorem A1.7). In the non-injective case, a measurable choice \(s(x)\in\mathrm H^{-1}(x)\) together with (F) also gives a measurable policy when \(\mathrm U\) is measurable. State these hypotheses rather than infer them from the diagram.

    Consistency with the transition. Candidate and coordinate names alone do not identify the solution of a Bellman problem. In a deterministic transition \(s=\mathrm g_\succ(x,u)\) the two rewritings of the identification (R5) denote the same relation when \(\mathrm g_\succ(\mathrm H(s),\mathrm U(s))=s\), with feasibility and the candidate's defining equations, and when every feasible solution of the explicit relation \(u=\mathrm U(\mathrm g_\succ(x,u))\) satisfies \(x=\mathrm H(\mathrm g_\succ(x,u))\). Otherwise the parameterized candidate relation may omit solutions of the explicit equation. These conditions must hold for retained state arguments as well.

    Both identities follow from R2's rearrangement where the transition is affine in the decision coordinate: solved for that coordinate, with the candidate in place of the control, the transition gives the coordinate line, and substituting in either direction gives the two identities. For the savings transition \(a=w-c\), solved as \(w=a+c\), and the coordinate map \(\varphi(a)=a+\mathrm f(a)\), both follow algebraically, and the two rewritings of the final line give the same relation on the same represented domain. This is not a rule that any three suitably named fields define a policy. The construction checks the coordinate line in this way: it solves the transition for the decision coordinate, puts the candidate in place of the control, and compares the result with the line by algebraic simplification, every declared function being an opaque symbol. A line that does not reduce to it, such as w[>] = a + 2*c[>], or that agrees with it only under an identity between declared functions, is refused under the rule grid-line-consistency, and the refusal names the line as written and the transition solved for the coordinate. Where a transition has several outputs, the check is made only where every other output's line is given; otherwise nothing is checked and nothing is recorded. An omitted candidate or coordinate definition cannot be supplied by an analytic assumption. A borrowing-boundary extension requires a separate condition or branch, such as the clause on the candidate line of §4.5; it need not be attached to one particular final-line spelling.

    A declared multiplier. A declaration μ[>] : ℝ₊ ⟂ a >= 0 in the body of a policy block adds the coordinate \(\mu\), with its declared set, to the functions the block defines at the continuation perch, and restricts the continuation state and \(\mu\) jointly to the block's observable set $$ A_\succ={(a,\mu)\in X_a\times\mathbb R_+ : a\geq0,\ \mu\,a=0}, $$ which holds the bound as written and the product of \(\mu\) with the bound's slack; the bound is held even where the declared set of \(a\) implies it. The functions the block reads, \(\mathrm v_\succ\) and its derivative \(\mathrm{dv}_\succ\) among them, keep their own coordinates. The candidate and coordinate maps of the identification are then maps on \(A_\succ\). For the consumption stage of §4.2 with the declaration added and the inverse Euler line written c[>] = 𝑑𝑢𝑖𝑛𝑣(D_{a}V[>] + μ[>]) they are $$ \mathrm U(a,\mu)=\mathrm{du}^{-1}(\mathrm{dv}_\succ(a)+\mu),\qquad \mathrm H(a,\mu)=a+\mathrm U(a,\mu), $$ and the identification, rewritten at the continuation perch, states \(\pi(\mathrm H(s))=\mathrm U(s)\) for every \(s\in A_\succ\), one statement quantified over the observable set, on the image \(\mathrm H(A_\succ)\). Condition (F), coverage of the decision domain and measurability are required on \(A_\succ\) as on \(\mathsf S\). The declaration forms no operation and no wire. Its bound may name continuation states, other multipliers of the block, parameters, numbers and inf; a bound naming a control, a decision or arrival state or a candidate is refused, since reading a control through its candidate would be an inference. A constraint on a control is therefore written with the clause on the first-order condition. A multiplier declared in the body of a block that is no policy block is an error (R1). The package does not yet check this (§5.3).

    Several unknowns. (i, b_n) = solve_{i, b_n}(r_1, r_2) denotes the relation in the product of the unknowns' sets. It determines a tuple of outputs where there is one solution or an explicit selection from the solutions, under the measurability conditions above.

    R11 Sub-transitions and the placement of expectations¶

    Written form. A transition written across several statements, one per intermediate quantity, and a value statement whose expectation binds the shock realized on one of them.

    Interpretation. Statements \(a_1=\mathrm g_1(x,c)\) and \(a=\mathrm g_2(a_1,\eta)\) give the typed transition \(\mathrm g_\succ(x,c,\eta)=\mathrm g_2(\mathrm g_1(x,c),\eta)\). The shock argument is retained; the unqualified composition \(\mathrm g_2\circ\mathrm g_1\) would omit it. Substituting into a continuation value gives \(\mathrm v_\succ(\mathrm g_2(\mathrm g_1(x,c),\eta))\).

    When an expectation over \(\eta\) is authored, its position and conditional law determine which intermediate function is formed. If the conditional law depends only on \(a_1\), that function can be written \(\mathrm w(a_1)=\int\mathrm v_\succ(\mathrm g_2(a_1,z)) K_\eta(a_1,\mathrm dz)\). If the law retains other state or control dependence, those arguments must remain too. An additional expectation or an exchange with maximization cannot be inferred solely from the shock's occurrence in a transition. Conditional expectations can be combined using the tower property only with the required nested conditioning information and integrability; nonlinear aggregation must stay in its authored order.

    Shock timing therefore constrains the validity of the written value operators; it does not insert them. An expectation is written in a statement of a backward block or of the policy block: a statement of a transition, an intermediate one included, takes no expectation, and a = a_1 + E_{η}(η) is refused under transition-without-expectation (R9). Intermediate functions remain part of the mathematical composition whether or not a !node gives one an independently addressable node. The package does not yet print the composition on the card of a builder whose intermediate line has no !node: the card then prints the rule of the intermediate line as the rule of the value (R5, §5.3). See Operators and builders §10.

    Recursive preferences. The order of expectation and any nonlinear transformation is part of the value equation's meaning. A certainty equivalent replaces a random continuation value with a certain one under the model's preference rule. This appendix defines no specialized form for that operation; the value statement must write the required transformations explicitly, with their domains and order preserved.

    R12 Envelopes¶

    Written form. A selection among branch values, (V_w[>] - δ, V_r[>])[d] with d = argmax_{d}(V_w[>] - δ, V_r[>]), whose maximand is the tuple of the value line, written entry by entry in the same order; an authored upper_env(…); or nothing, when an envelope is a step inside a numerical method. A maximand that is another tuple is refused under branch-tuple-mismatch, also where it holds the same entries in another order or an entry equal in value but written differently, such as -δ + V_w[>]. A value line that selects by no tuple indexed by d, such as V = max(V_w[>] - δ, V_r[>]), is refused under the same rule.

    Interpretation. A finite branch maximum and a selection among a parameterized set of candidates are different uses of an upper envelope. For branches, the value is \(\max_j Q(x,j)\) and a selected label attains it. For the relation of R10, define the feasible fiber \(S_x=\{s:\mathrm H(s)=x,\ \mathrm U(s)\in\mathcal C(x)\}\). The candidate value and selected control are $$ \overline V(x)=\sup_{s\in S_x}Q(x,\mathrm U(s)),\qquad s^(x)\in\operatornameQ(x,\mathrm U(s)),\qquad \pi(x)=\mathrm U(s^*(x)). $$ The maximization selects a parameter }_{s\in S_x\(s^*\); the policy is its control \(\mathrm U(s^*)\), not the parameter itself. Attainment and a measurable selection are required for the latter two expressions. A non-injective coordinate map need not give different controls in a fiber (condition (F)), and concavity alone does not make an arbitrarily parameterized map injective.

    An authored upper_env requests the stated mathematical operation. The package does not yet give it that definition: it reads the line, lets a method such as !FUES address the operator, checks none of its arguments, and lists the condition external-callable-signature-and-provider for it (§5.3). A numerical method may compare interpolated candidate values as part of realizing a declared optimization problem, without creating an authored upper_env, evaluate, or !node statement. An envelope between finite samples is generally an approximation, not a proof of the exact maximum.

    Assumptions. Selecting by value requires the value of each candidate; marginal values alone generally do not suffice. Including a global maximizer is sufficient for equality with the full Bellman maximum. If the candidate supremum is attained, equality also requires an included global maximizer; an unattained supremum can equal the optimum value without supplying an optimal control. Differentiability makes the first-order condition necessary at an interior maximum, but does not prove that a particular candidate parameterization covers all such roots. Binding constraints, corners, and any missing branches need their own candidates or conditions. A numerical envelope cannot supply missing equations or establish global optimality by itself.

    4.1 A product output without consumption¶

    A firm with capital and debt

    A firm arrives with capital \(k\) and debt \(b\), observes a productivity shock \(z\), chooses investment \(i\) and new borrowing \(b_n\), and pays out what remains. The continuation state is the pair (capital, debt).

    @stage: firm

α : (0,1)
β : (0,1)
δ : [0,1]
r : ℝ₊
b_max : ℝ₊₊
μ_z : ℝ
σ_z : ℝ₊

z : ℝ₊₊
z ~ LogNormal(μ_z, σ_z)

𝑓(k : ℝ₊, z : ℝ₊₊) := z*k^α                                    :: [id=production]
𝑑𝑖𝑣(k : ℝ₊, b : ℝ₊, z : ℝ₊₊, i : ℝ₊, b_n : ℝ₊) := 𝑓(k, z) - i - (1+r)*b + b_n   :: [id=payout]

[id=arvl_to_dcsn, (k : ℝ₊, b : ℝ₊, z) : !arvl -> (k_d : ℝ₊, b_d : ℝ₊, z_d : ℝ₊₊) : !dcsn] {
    k_d = k
    b_d = b
    z_d = z
}

[id=dcsn_to_cntn, (k_d, b_d, i : !ctrl @ ℝ₊, b_n : !ctrl @ [0, b_max]) : !dcsn -> (k[>] : ℝ₊, b[>] : ℝ₊) : !cntn] {
    k[>] = (1-δ)*k_d + i
    b[>] = b_n
}

[id=cntn_to_dcsn, (V[>] : ℝ @ !vlu, i, b_n) : !cntn -> (V : ℝ @ !vlu) : !dcsn] {
    V = 𝑑𝑖𝑣(k_d, b_d, z_d, i, b_n) + β*V[>]        :: [id=value]
}

[id=dcsn_to_arvl, (V) : !dcsn -> (V[<] : ℝ @ !vlu) : !arvl] {
    V[<] = E_{z}(V)                                :: [id=value]
}

[id=policy, (V[>] : ℝ @ !vlu) : !cntn -> (i, b_n) : !dcsn] {
    (i, b_n) = argmax_{i, b_n}(𝑑𝑖𝑣(k_d, b_d, z_d, i, b_n) + β*V[>])   :: [id=choice]
}

    Context (§2.4). Parameters \(\alpha, \beta, \delta, r, b_{\max}, \mu_z, \sigma_z\). The shock \(z\) is taken to have the displayed law conditionally on arrival information. This is a constant conditional law and hence states independence from that information; the numerical parameters alone would not imply it. The arrival state \(X_\prec = (k, b)\), the decision state \(X_\sim = (k_d, b_d, z_d)\), the controls \(i \in \mathbb R_+\) and \(b_n \in [0, b_{\max}]\), the continuation state \(X_\succ = (k_\succ, b_\succ)\) with the two coordinates the second block's output side lists (R3). Two declared functions, each binding its formal arguments.

    R1. The first block is a forward transition from \(\prec\) to \(\sim\) with the shock as an argument, \(\mathrm g_\prec(k, b, z) = (k, b, z)\): the realized shock becomes a coordinate of the decision state. The second is the forward transition \(\mathrm g_\succ(k_d, b_d, i, b_n) = ((1-\delta) k_d + i,\; b_n)\), with two coordinate functions (R3). The third and fourth are backward blocks; the fifth is a policy block, because its output is a pair of controls.

    R6, scope. The value statement reads \(k_d, b_d, z_d\), the coordinates of the decision state, at which the value it defines stands: they are coordinates of the output function, and the header does not list them (R1). The header lists the continuation value V[>], the one input of the block, and the controls \(i, b_n\), at which its lines are read; the controls are at the decision perch (§2.2).

    R5. With \(V_\succ = \mathrm v_\succ(k_\succ, b_\succ)\) and the transition substituted, $$ Q(k, b, z, i, b_n) = \mathrm{div}(k, b, z, i, b_n) + \beta\,\mathrm v_\succ\bigl((1-\delta) k + i,\; b_n\bigr), $$ the choice value with both controls retained.

    R8 and R7. The policy block binds \(i\) and \(b_n\) jointly. Its line, which is not marked !doc, denotes the set of the maximizing policies (R8): at each state the pairs that maximize \(Q\) over \(\mathbb R_+ \times [0, b_{\max}]\) form the maximizer set, and a policy is a measurable selection from it, \((i, b_n) = \pi(k, b, z) \in \arg\max\). The interpretation assumes that the maximum is attained. The stage records no condition for it: stage.obligations() lists no condition of attainment beside the operation of the kind Argmax that the line forms, and the set of the maximizing policies may be empty (R7). The bound \(b_{\max}\) is what makes the feasible set compact in the borrowing direction, and attainment in the investment direction requires a separate restriction. For example, continuity and an objective tending to \(-\infty\) as investment grows, uniformly over the compact borrowing interval, suffice. A marginal value merely below unit cost without a uniform tail condition is not by itself an attainment proof. Then \(\mathrm v_\sim(k, b, z) = Q(k, b, z, \pi(k, b, z))\).

    R7, expectation. In V[<] = E_{z}(V) the shock \(z\) is bound and \(k, b\) are free. Since \(V = \mathrm v_\sim(k, b, z)\) with \((k, b)\) measurable at arrival and \(z\) independent of the arrival information, $$ \mathrm v_\prec(k, b) = \int \mathrm v_\sim(k, b, z)\,\mu_z(\mathrm dz), $$ provided that, at each \((k, b)\), the integrand \(\mathrm v_\sim(k, b, \cdot)\) is integrable under the law of \(z\), so that the integral is finite, as the declaration of V[<] in \(\mathbb R\) requires (R7). The stage records this requirement as the condition expectation-integrability, which stage.obligations() lists as required.

    The stage operator. \(\mathbb T : \mathrm v_\succ \mapsto \mathrm v_\prec\) is the composite of these four steps: substitute \(\mathrm g_\succ\), add the payout, select, integrate over \(z\).

    R9. Forward, a law \(\mu_\prec\) of \((k, b)\) becomes the product \(\mu_\prec \otimes \mu_z\) on the decision state, and then the law of \(((1-\delta)k + \pi_i(k, b, z),\; \pi_{b_n}(k, b, z))\) on the continuation state, a joint law of the pair and not a product of marginals.

    4.2 The consumption stage and the discounting convention¶

    The stage cons is the consumption step of a two-stage period whose other stage, disc, carries the discount factor.

    @stage: cons

Xm := ℝ₊₊
Xa := ℝ₊

ρ : [1, inf)

𝑢(c : ℝ₊₊) := c^(1-ρ)/(1-ρ)         :: [id=util]
𝑑𝑢(c : ℝ₊₊) := c^(-ρ)               :: [id=marg_util]
𝑑𝑢𝑖𝑛𝑣(x : ℝ₊₊) := x^(-1/ρ)          :: [id=inv_marg_util]

[id=arvl_to_dcsn, (m : Xm) : !arvl -> (m_d : Xm) : !dcsn] {
    m_d = m
}

[id=dcsn_to_cntn, (m_d, c : !ctrl @ ℝ₊) : !dcsn -> (a : Xa) : !cntn] {

    4.3 Identifiers are addresses¶

    The reading of §4.2 used the identifiers InvEuler, endoggrid, g_cntn_to_dcsn, and cons_choice nowhere. What it used were the perch marks and the roles: c[>] is the output c read at the continuation perch, defined by the first statement; w[>] is the decision coordinate w read at the continuation perch, defined by the second; and the third statement identifies the output with its continuation-perch version, its right side depending on the output through the transition a = w - c (R10). Renaming the three identifiers to first, second, and third, and updating the methods file that addresses them, leaves every denotation unchanged (R1).

    The final line states one relation, which R5 rewrites at either perch. Read at the decision perch, c = c[>] is \(c = \mathrm f(w - c)\), an implicit equation that a root finder can solve without the coordinate map. The package does not yet form this reading. It reads a block that writes the candidate line and the final line without the coordinate line, and the card then states no relation for the final line (R5, §5.3). Rewritten at the continuation perch, the second statement supplying the decision coordinate w read there (R5 (A3)), the final line is \(\pi(\varphi(a)) = \mathrm f(a)\), the relation the construction states, since that perch's map contains no unknown. The mark on w[>] identifies that coordinate; the budget identity and the equations establish the transition consistency required by R10, which the construction checks. A mark or an identifier alone cannot establish that consistency. A block without the final line states no policy and is refused (R1).

    4.4 The perch form and the functional form of one statement¶

    The arrival block of cons_savings_iid writes, in the perch form,

    V[<] = E_{y}(V)

    Step 1, the fields. V[<] denotes the value field at arrival, V the value field at decision, y the income shock, realized on the edge from arrival to decision.

    Step 2, binding (R4). Inside E_{y}(…) the shock y is bound. The free names of the statement are those V depends on other than y: by R5 applied to the decision value, \(V = \mathrm v_\sim(w)\) with \(w = y + R\,b\), so the remaining state argument is \(b\). The supplied function \(\mathrm v_\sim\), the return \(R\), and the parameters of the shock law remain inputs or parameters; binding the shock does not bind that function.

    Step 3, the conditional expectation (R7). The left side is at the arrival perch, so E_{y} denotes \(\mathbb E[\,\cdot \mid \mathcal F_\prec]\). The integrand is \(\mathrm v_\sim(y + R\,b)\), a function of the \(\mathcal F_\prec\)-measurable \(b\) and the shock \(y\), whose displayed law is here assumed to be its conditional law given \(\mathcal F_\prec\). Its constancy in the arrival state is a conditional independence assertion, not an inference from a marginal distribution. By the disintegration theorem, $$ V_\prec = \mathbb E\bigl[\mathrm v_\sim(y + R\,b) \,\big|\, \mathcal F_\prec\bigr] = \int \mathrm v_\sim(y' + R\,b)\,\nu_y(\mathrm dy') \quad\text{almost surely.} $$

    Step 4, the functional form (R5). The right side is a function of \(b\) alone, so it represents \(V_\prec\) with respect to \(X_\prec = b\): $$ \mathrm v_\prec(b) = \int \mathrm v_\sim(y + R\,b)\,\nu_y(\mathrm dy), \qquad \nu_y = p_0\,\delta_0 + (1 - p_0)\,\mathrm{LogNormal}(\mu_y, \sigma_y). $$

    Here \(\nu_y\) names the probability law, \(\mu_y\) is the lognormal location parameter written μ_y, \(p_0\) is p_zero, and \(\delta_0\) is unit probability at zero. This avoids using one symbol for both a law and its numerical parameter.

    Step 5, the derivative line. The methods file's block dcsn_to_arvl writes D_{b}V[<] = E_{y}(R * D_{w}V), which lowers in the same way to \(\mathrm{dv}_\prec(b) = R \int \mathrm{dv}_\sim(y + R\,b)\,\nu_y(\mathrm dy)\): D_{w}V denotes \(\mathrm{dv}_\sim\), the derivative of the decision value in \(w\), and the factor \(R\) is the derivative of the law \(w = y + R\,b\) in \(b\). The package does not yet record this reading: the rule that it prints for the line differentiates in \(w\) the decision value composed with the law, \(\mathrm v_\sim(y + R\,b)\), which does not depend on \(w\), at a point \(w\) that the rule leaves free (§1.5, §5.3). The line states the derivative of the arrival value in \(b\), and it is that derivative when differentiation under the integral sign is justified, an assumption the rules record and nothing checks (R8). The author writes the line because the law between the perches is not the identity and carries the factor \(R\). Where the law is the identity, as in V[<] = V of §4.2, no derivative line is written: the derivative of the arrival value is the derivative of the decision value.

    What the functional form must not say. The expectation acts on the field, that is on the function evaluated at the state \(y + R\,b\) that contains the bound shock. The expression \(\mathbb E_y\,\mathrm v_\sim\), an expectation of the function itself, does not denote the arrival value, and no reading of it recovers the evaluation the perch form carries implicitly (R5). A functional-form spelling of this statement must therefore be \(\mathrm v_\prec(b) = \mathbb E_y\,\mathrm v_\sim(y + R\,b)\), with the argument written.

    4.5 An implicit statement with a constrained policy¶

    Under the concavity and endpoint hypotheses stated below, the Euler equality has a feasible solution for \(w\geq\mathrm f(0)\), with positive saving for \(w>\mathrm f(0)\). Below that threshold the borrowing constraint binds. The following two-block fragment states the transition and constrained policy relation on \(w>0\); it does not supply the complete value stage. The power-utility body also requires \(\gamma\neq1\).

    @stage: cons_constrained

β : (0,1)
γ : ℝ₊₊

𝑢(c : ℝ₊₊) := c^(1-γ)/(1-γ)         :: [id=util]
𝑑𝑢     := D_{c}(𝑢)                   :: [id=marg_util]
𝑑𝑢𝑖𝑛𝑣 := Inv(𝑑𝑢)                    :: [id=inv_marg_util]

[id=dcsn_to_cntn, (w : ℝ₊₊, c : !ctrl @ ℝ₊₊) : !dcsn -> (a : ℝ₊) : !cntn] {
    a = w - c
}

[id=policy, (V[>] : ℝ @ !vlu) : !cntn -> (c : ℝ₊₊ @ !ctrl) : !dcsn] {
    c[>] = 𝑑𝑢𝑖𝑛𝑣(β*D_{a}V[>])        :: [id=candidate]
    w[>] = a + c[>]                  :: [id=coordinate]
    𝑑𝑢(c) = β*D_{a}V[>] ⟂ w - c >= 0 :: [id=euler]
}

    R10. The third statement has an expression on its left, and its unknown is the block's output \(c\). Its clause writes w - c, the expression for the continuation state supplied by the forward block. After the substitution of R5, under which D_{a}V[>] denotes \(\mathrm{dv}_\succ(a)\), the derivative of \(\mathrm v_\succ\) at the saving \(a = w - c\), the residual is \(r(w, c) = \mathrm{du}(c) - \beta\,\mathrm{dv}_\succ(w - c)\). The package does not yet record this residual (R10, §5.3). The clause denotes $$ r(w, c) \ge 0, \qquad w - c \ge 0, \qquad r(w, c)\,(w - c) = 0 . $$ Where saving is positive the Euler equation holds with equality; where the agent saves nothing, \(c = w\), marginal utility may exceed the discounted marginal value of saving. This is the Kuhn–Tucker system of \(\max_{0 < c \le w} \{\mathrm u(c) + \beta\,\mathrm v_\succ(w - c)\}\).

    The solution under concavity. Suppose \(\mathrm u\) is strictly concave and continuously differentiable with \(\mathrm{du}\) a decreasing bijection of \(\mathbb R_{++}\), and \(\mathrm v_\succ\) is continuously differentiable with \(\mathrm{dv}_\succ\) positive, continuous, and nonincreasing. Then \(\mathrm f = \mathrm{du}^{-1} \circ (\beta\,\mathrm{dv}_\succ)\) is continuous and nondecreasing, \(\varphi = \mathrm{id} + \mathrm f\) is continuous and strictly increasing with range \([\mathrm f(0), \infty)\), the fiber condition holds on that range, and the system has exactly one solution at every \(w > 0\): $$ \pi^\ast(w) = \begin{cases} w, & 0 < w \le \mathrm f(0), \ \mathrm f(\varphi^{-1}(w)), & w > \mathrm f(0). \end{cases} $$ This \(\pi^\ast\) is the maximizer, because the objective is strictly concave in \(c\) and the Kuhn–Tucker conditions are then sufficient. Without the clause the statement 𝑑𝑢(c) = β*D_{a}V[>] denotes the interior relation only, which has no solution below \(\mathrm f(0)\); a method that returns \(c = w\) there is solving a problem the file has not declared, and the rules require it to report the extension rather than to return it silently.

    Equality and complementarity are different. On the candidate relation, the equalities 𝑑𝑢(c) = β*D_{a}V[>], c = c[>], and w = w[>] have the same roots. They cannot all carry the same complementarity sign. Since \(\mathrm{du}\) is strictly decreasing, $$ \mathrm{du}(c)-\beta\,\mathrm{dv}_\succ(a)\geq0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathrm f(a)-c\geq0. $$ The residual of c = c[>] is instead \(c-\mathrm f(a)\), with the opposite sign. The coordinate residual with the correct sign is \(\varphi(a)-w=\mathrm f(a)-c\) when \(a=w-c\). Thus converting an Euler complementarity condition into a candidate or coordinate condition requires orienting the residual correctly; equivalence of the underlying equalities is insufficient.

    The declared multiplier. The same constraint can be written with its multiplier declared (R10). The policy block is then

    [id=policy, (V[>] : ℝ @ !vlu) : !cntn -> (c : ℝ₊₊ @ !ctrl) : !dcsn] {
    μ[>] : ℝ₊ ⟂ a >= 0                 :: [id=slackness]
    c[>] = 𝑑𝑢𝑖𝑛𝑣(β*D_{a}V[>] + μ[>])   :: [id=candidate]
    w[>] = a + c[>]                    :: [id=coordinate]
    c    = c[>]                        :: [id=choice]
}

    Its candidate and coordinate maps are defined on the observable set \(A_\succ = \{(a, \mu) \in \mathbb R_+ \times \mathbb R_+ : a \ge 0,\ \mu\, a = 0\}\): \(\mathrm U(a, \mu) = \mathrm{du}^{-1}(\beta\,\mathrm{dv}_\succ(a) + \mu)\) and \(\mathrm H(a, \mu) = a + \mathrm U(a, \mu)\), and the block states \(\pi(\mathrm H(s)) = \mathrm U(s)\) for every \(s \in A_\succ\).

    The clause on the candidate line. The same constraint can be written as a clause on the candidate line. The policy block is then

    [id=policy, (V[>] : ℝ @ !vlu) : !cntn -> (c : ℝ₊₊ @ !ctrl) : !dcsn] {
    c[>] <= 𝑑𝑢𝑖𝑛𝑣(β*D_{a}V[>]) ⟂ a >= 0   :: [id=candidate]
    w[>] = a + c[>]                      :: [id=coordinate]
    c    = c[>]                          :: [id=choice]
}

    After an inequality the residual takes the sign of that relation (R10), so the clause admits at each saving \(a\) the values \(x \in \mathbb R_{++}\) with \(x - \mathrm f(a) \le 0\), \(a \ge 0\) and \((x - \mathrm f(a))\,a = 0\): the one value \(\mathrm f(a)\) where \(a > 0\), and every \(x\) in \((0, \mathrm f(0)]\) where \(a = 0\). The coordinate line gives each admitted value its cash on hand \(a + x\), and the final line, rewritten at the continuation perch (R5), states \(\pi(a + x) = x\) for every saving \(a\) and every value \(x\) admitted there: \(\pi(\varphi(a)) = \mathrm f(a)\) where \(a > 0\), and \(\pi(x) = x\) for \(0 < x \le \mathrm f(0)\), the corner.

    The three spellings denote one relation. Assume that 𝑑𝑢𝑖𝑛𝑣 is the inverse of 𝑑𝑢 on \(\mathbb R_{++}\), authored as in §4.2 or derived by Inv as here, and that the saving is \(a = w - c\). Then the clause of the first block and the declared multiplier of the second denote one relation between \(w\) and \(c\). For \((a, \mu) \in A_\succ\) put \(c = \mathrm U(a, \mu)\) and \(w = a + c\): then \(w - c = a \ge 0\), \(r(w, c) = \mu \ge 0\) and \(r(w, c)\,(w - c) = \mu\, a = 0\), which is the system of the clause. Conversely, a solution \((w, c)\) of that system gives \(a = w - c\) and \(\mu = r(w, c)\), a point of \(A_\succ\) with \(\mathrm H(a, \mu) = w\) and \(\mathrm U(a, \mu) = c\). If further \(\mathrm{du}\) is strictly decreasing, the clause on the candidate line of the third block denotes the same relation, and its pairs are those of the declared multiplier: the grid pair \((\varphi(a), \mathrm f(a))\) at a saving \(a > 0\) is the pair at \((a, 0)\), and the corner pair \((x, x)\) with \(0 < x \le \mathrm f(0)\) is the pair at \((0, \mu)\) with \(\mu = \mathrm{du}(x) - \beta\,\mathrm{dv}_\succ(0)\), which is nonnegative exactly when \(x \le \mathrm f(0)\) because \(\mathrm{du}\) is strictly decreasing. Conversely the pair at \((0, \mu)\) is the corner pair at \(x = \mathrm U(0, \mu) \le \mathrm f(0)\). When \(\mathrm{du}\) is a decreasing bijection of \(\mathbb R_{++}\), the map \(x \mapsto \mathrm{du}(x) - \beta\,\mathrm{dv}_\succ(0)\) carries \((0, \mathrm f(0)]\) onto \([0, \infty)\). The inequality statement written without the clause, c[>] <= 𝑑𝑢𝑖𝑛𝑣(β*D_{a}V[>]), its relation read as the equality on the grid, denotes the part of the relation with \(\mu = 0\) and is no spelling of the condition. The identity of the first block with the second uses no monotonicity and no concavity; that of the third uses the monotonicity of \(\mathrm{du}\) and no concavity.

    One policy, the maximizer. Suppose further (H1) that \(\mathrm u\) is strictly concave and differentiable with \(\mathrm{du}\) a decreasing bijection of \(\mathbb R_{++}\), (H1′) that 𝑑𝑢𝑖𝑛𝑣 is its inverse, (H2) that \(\mathrm{dv}_\succ : \mathbb R_+ \to \mathbb R_{++}\) is continuous and nonincreasing, \(\mathrm{dv}_\succ(0)\) being the right derivative of \(\mathrm v_\succ\) at zero, and (H3) that the transition is \(\mathrm g_\succ(w, c) = w - c\). Let \(\bar w = \mathrm f(0) = \mathrm{du}^{-1}(\beta\,\mathrm{dv}_\succ(0))\).

    1. The residual \(r(w, c) = \mathrm{du}(c) - \beta\,\mathrm{dv}_\succ(w - c)\) is strictly decreasing in \(c\) on \((0, w]\) and tends to \(+\infty\) as \(c \to 0\), so the clause's system has one solution at every \(w > 0\): \(c = w\), with multiplier \(r(w, w) \ge 0\), when \(w \le \bar w\), and the unique interior root otherwise. The first block defines the policy \(\pi^\ast\) above.
    2. \(\mathrm H\) is a bijection of \(A_\succ\) onto \(\mathbb R_{++}\). On the half-line \(\mu = 0\) it is \(\varphi\), strictly increasing with range \([\bar w, \infty)\); on the half-line \(a = 0\) it is \(\mu \mapsto \mathrm{du}^{-1}(\beta\,\mathrm{dv}_\succ(0) + \mu)\), strictly decreasing with range \((0, \bar w]\); the two meet at \((0, 0) \mapsto \bar w\). The half-line \(a = 0\) reaches every \(w\) below \(\bar w\) because the declared set of \(\mu\), \(\mathbb R_+\), is unbounded.
    3. The policy of the second block, \(\mathrm U \circ \mathrm H^{-1}\), equals \(\pi^\ast\), by the identity of relations with items 1 and 2, and so does the policy the third block states, \(\mathrm f \circ \varphi^{-1}\) on \([\bar w, \infty)\) and the identity on \((0, \bar w]\).
    4. Both are the maximizer of \(\mathrm u(c) + \beta\,\mathrm v_\succ(w - c)\) over \((0, w]\) when \(\mathrm v_\succ\) is concave with derivative \(\mathrm{dv}_\succ\): for a concave objective and a linear constraint the Kuhn–Tucker conditions are sufficient (Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004, §5.5.3), the conditions being those of Nocedal and Wright (2006, Theorem 12.1). Dobrescu and Shanker (2024, Proposition 3.1) state the corresponding result for the sequence problem, given a fixed and optimal sequence of future discrete choices; their Definition 1 names the property that roots coincide with solutions and proves nothing. Sufficiency is a property of the stage's problem: it holds or fails whichever method computes the policy. For the three blocks above, written in a stage file, it is stated here, on this page, and the stage records no condition for it. Where the endogenous grid method is typed on the policy builder, stage.obligations() lists the condition egm-eligibility (R8), that the continuation value is concave and continuously differentiable on \((0,\infty)\) with a finite positive right derivative at zero, and nothing checks it.

    In general. Uniqueness and optimality of a root rest on concavity of the continuation value, equivalently \(\mathrm{dv}_\succ\) nonincreasing, with strict concavity of \(\mathrm u\); uniqueness of the multiplier given a root rests on the rank condition of Dobrescu and Shanker (2024, Assumption 3.3). Their Theorem 4.1 gives only local existence of the inverse near an interior solution in one constrained region, a piece of the observable set (their equation (5)); existence on a whole region needs the Poincaré–Miranda theorem (their §4.2 and online appendix, Corollary F.1) or, in one dimension, item 2. By their Claims 4.1 and 4.2 the grid a method inverts from has on each region the dimension of the state, one coordinate here: the saving on one half-line of \(A_\succ\) and the multiplier on the other. Condition (F), coverage and measurability are stated hypotheses (R10), injectivity being one stronger sufficient condition. Where the continuation value is not concave, the case their paper is written for, the three spellings still denote one relation but none defines a function: several roots stand at one state, and a method selects among them. For the third, two admitted pairs may then share one cash on hand with different candidates, and the stage states the relation and no selection among them. The rules check none of this.

    4.6 The endogenous-grid policy with and without its final line¶

    Take the policy block of §4.2 in its two forms.

    With the final line. c = c[>] gives \(\{(w,c):c=\mathrm f(w-c),\ w-c\geq0\}\) on the declared control domain. By the budget identity this is the candidate relation below. Under condition (F), coverage of the chosen domain \(D\), and measurability, it defines a policy \(\pi\) on \(D\). In this savings example, injectivity of \(\varphi\) permits \(\pi=\mathrm f\circ\varphi^{-1}\).

    Without the final line. The candidate and coordinate equations give the pairs \(P=\{(\varphi(a),\mathrm f(a)):a\in\mathbb R_+\}\), but no line states the policy, and the block is refused (R1). The final line is what states the relation on those pairs, rewritten at the continuation perch as \(\pi(\varphi(a)) = \mathrm f(a)\) (R10). The identities \(\varphi(a)-\mathrm f(a)=a\) and \(w=(w-c)+\mathrm f(w-c)\) for any explicit solution establish both directions of R10's consistency requirement, so the two readings of the final line give the same relation on the same domain. It is these equations and their declared types that justify the rewriting, not the conventional names of the aliases.

    This relation does not by itself include every constrained corner. The boundary \(c=w\) below the threshold requires the correctly oriented condition of §4.5, the declared multiplier of §4.5, the clause on the candidate line of §4.5, or a separately declared boundary rule or branch. That condition need not be attached to one particular final-line spelling. In a more general model, the candidate and coordinate maps must specify the full required state dependence, satisfy the transition and feasibility conditions, and cover the solutions being claimed. Three perch/role labels alone do not establish those premises.

    What the method adds. The endogenous grid method chooses an asset grid \(a_1 < \dots < a_N\), tabulates the pairs \((\varphi(a_i), \mathrm f(a_i))\), and takes as its approximate policy the function through those points, with a stated extrapolation beyond the last knot (the endogenous grid method of Carroll 2006). When the file writes the clause on the first-order condition, declares the multiplier, or writes the clause on the candidate line (§4.5), the segment \(c = w\) on \((0, \varphi(0)]\) is not something the method adds: it belongs to the exact relation the file states (§4.5, items 1 and 3), and the method computes it with the other points, by the segment to the origin, on the multiplier's half-line of the observable set, or at the values the clause on the candidate line admits at zero saving. Without any of the three, the segment lies outside the declared relation, and a method that returns it reports an extension (§4.5). If the method uses an interpolant, it agrees with its computed pairs at the knots. Those pairs equal the exact policy only when the supplied continuation values and candidate calculations are exact. Increasing knots are a diagnostic, not a proof of global injectivity or condition (F): behavior between sampled points is untested. The construction records none of these conditions: whether one holds, is established under the hypotheses of §4.5, is shown by a numerical diagnostic without such a proof, or is unverified is a statement about the problem and the method, made on no record of the stage.

    An explicit evaluation. The author may instead write c = evaluate(w[>], c[>], w). With exact coordinate and candidate maps, and the functional interpretation of R10, this evaluates the same policy. With tabulated pairs the line evaluates the chosen interpolating representation, which generally approximates that policy. The package does not yet construct either reading: the rule of the line prints UNRESOLVED[evaluate; mathematical definition], also with !Linear typed at the line (R8, §5.3). Distinct knots alone neither choose an interpolant nor establish the exact policy between them (R7).

    4.7 A discrete choice and the pension model¶

    A discrete choice is written as a block whose one control is valued in a finite set of labels and whose output side is empty, with one nested block per label, in the order in which the set lists the labels; nested blocks in another order are refused under branch-labels. The backward block selects among the branch values by the control. Each continuation value stands at one position of the tuple, and that position decides the branch to which the value belongs; a value at two positions or at none is refused under branch-value-position. The policy block chooses the control by maximizing over the same tuple in the same order (R12):

    @stage: ret_choice

δ : ℝ₊

[id=arvl_to_dcsn, (a : ℝ₊) : !arvl -> (a_d : ℝ₊) : !dcsn] {
    a_d = a
}

[id=dcsn_to_cntn, (a_d, d : !ctrl @ {work, retire}) : !dcsn -> ()] {
    [label=work,   () -> (a_w : ℝ₊) : !cntn] { a_w = a_d }
    [label=retire, () -> (a_r : ℝ₊) : !cntn] { a_r = a_d }
}

[id=cntn_to_dcsn, (V_w[>] : ℝ @ !vlu, V_r[>] : ℝ @ !vlu, d) : !cntn -> (V : ℝ @ !vlu) : !dcsn] {
    V = (V_w[>] - δ, V_r[>])[d]        :: [id=choice_value]
}

[id=dcsn_to_arvl, (V) : !dcsn -> (V[<] : ℝ @ !vlu) : !arvl] {
    V[<] = V
}

[id=policy, (V_w[>], V_r[>]) -> (d)] {
    d = argmax_{d}(V_w[>] - δ, V_r[>])  :: [id=choice]
}

    R3. The continuation state is the disjoint union \(\mathsf X_\succ = \mathbb R_+ \sqcup \mathbb R_+\), one copy per label, each nested block defining the coordinates of its copy (Branching). The tuple (V_w[>] - δ, V_r[>]) is a family in label order, and [d] selects the entry the control names.

    R5, R8, R12. With \(V_{w,\succ} = \mathrm v_w(a_w)\) and \(V_{r,\succ} = \mathrm v_r(a_r)\) and both branch transitions the identity, the choice value is \(Q(a, d) = (\mathrm v_w(a) - \delta,\ \mathrm v_r(a))[d]\), a selected policy \(\pi(a) \in \arg\max_d Q(a, d)\) with a tie-break the rules do not fix, and \(\mathrm v_\sim(a) = \max\{\mathrm v_w(a) - \delta, \mathrm v_r(a)\}\), the exact branch maximum. Forward, a state \(a\) is routed to the copy \(\pi(a)\) names (R9).

    The pension model. The savings-and-retirement model of Druedahl and Jørgensen (2017), with a contribution cap, as solved by the inverse Euler method of Dobrescu and Shanker, combines the constructions above. Its worker stage has a decision state with three coordinates, liquid assets, pension assets and earnings, two controls, consumption and the pension deposit, and a continuation state with two coordinates, so both transitions are products (R3); its retirement choice is a branch of the form just shown; its earnings shock is realized between arrival and decision, so the expectation over it stands in the arrival block, V[<] = E_{w}(V) in dcsn_to_arvl, as in §4.4 (R7); and its first-order conditions, one per asset, form one system in two unknowns, which R10 reads as a solve over both, whose residuals are subject to the three constraints the model imposes, the liquidity constraint on the liquid asset, the no-withdrawal constraint on the pension, and the cap, with their appropriate inequality directions and multipliers in a coupled constrained system. R10's scalar lower-bound example is not a complete derivation of that system. Discrete retirement choice can create non-concavities and competing candidates; neither policy non-monotonicity nor non-injectivity of a particular coordinate map follows from the branch syntax alone. When candidates overlap and the required coverage and selection conditions hold, R12 describes selection by value; an upper-envelope method approximates that operation. The application's stage files stand under applications/pension-retirement/declaration/stages/: worker_saving.bl states the worker's maximization, the block policy of its methods file, applications/pension-retirement/declaration/stages/worker_saving/methods.bl, writes its two first-order conditions with the clauses ⟂ m_f - c - c_p >= 0 and ⟂ 0 <= c_p <= w_d of R10 under time iteration, and applications/pension-retirement/economics.md records their correspondence with the paper's equations.

    5.2 What the rules do not decide¶

    The rules assign no numerical method. Quadrature for an expectation, interpolation for an evaluation, the grid on which a policy is tabulated, and the envelope step of a method attach afterwards (Methodization). The grid on which a problem is solved and the grid on which a population is simulated are two numerical choices with separate accuracy criteria, and neither is part of the declared sets or laws. Which quantities are recorded along a simulated path is a matter for the simulation and not for the stage.

    5.3 Open points¶

    • A functional-form spelling of a value statement, with the state arguments written, is intended to be admitted under the same context as the perch form (§1.4); which such spellings the grammar admits is a separate question from what they mean.
    • A tuple law can specify a joint distribution. Separate declarations on one edge are read as the product of §2.1, which the elaboration records as the condition joint-distribution-factorization. Any unsupported conditional-law spelling must be distinguished from the mathematical conditional kernel it intends.
    • A specialized form for a certainty equivalent under recursive preferences remains outside these rules; explicit transformations are read in their written order (R11).
