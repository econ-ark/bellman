    declarative language¶

    A language is declarative to the degree that a text written in it states what an object is, through its sets, probability distributions, equations and choices; takes its meaning from the meanings of its parts, with no appeal to an order of execution; and leaves the procedure that computes the object to be supplied separately. The three clauses are tested against published sources in the sections below. The phrase "to the degree" is deliberate: the modeling languages economists use occupy different positions on this scale, and the Stan and BUGS comparison below shows two of them.

    Bellman-SYM is declarative in this sense. A stage file states the domains, information, choices, equations and shock probability distributions of one stage. Elaboration, the step that reads the stage files, assigns each file its meaning, the semgraph, which records the operators the stage denotes and holds no procedure for computing them. The routines that compute the stage are supplied afterwards, under methodization, and the methods file "names computations that the stage file created; it creates none" (Methodization).

    The style it is contrasted with¶

    Backus (1978, §4, pp. 615–616) explains the conventional imperative style by its origin in the machine: "Von Neumann programming languages use variables to imitate the computer's storage cells; control statements elaborate its jump and test instructions; and assignment statements imitate its fetching, storing, and arithmetic." The assignment statement "is the von Neumann bottleneck of programming languages and keeps us thinking in word-at-a-time terms in much the same way the computer's bottleneck does." Of the functional program for the inner product, Backus records (§5.2, p. 617) that "It operates only on its arguments. There are no hidden states or complex transition rules" and that "It is static and nonrepetitive, in the sense that its structure is helpful in understanding it without mentally executing it."

    The last property is the one an economist recognizes. The Bellman equation of the buffer-stock problem, as a paper writes it, is read without being run. A value-iteration loop that solves it must be traced, and in the project's interoperability talk the procedural approach is summarized by the observation that "the loop is the model", so that "changing the method or reusing one piece means editing the loop". A declarative text names the object; a procedure produces one approximation to it.

    Bandali (2020, §1.1, p. 2) characterizes declarative modeling languages as those that "describe the transitions in a declarative manner using constraints, rather than through imperative calculations and/or statements" and "have a formal mathematical and logical foundation". The thesis's subjects are software specification languages (TLA+, Alloy, Event-B); the characterization applies equally to a model file whose equations are constraints on functions.

    What is declared, and what is supplied separately¶

    Stan is the published case closest to the separation of a semgraph from its numerical realization. The Stan Reference Manual (Stan Development Team 2025, Overview, p. 1) states that "The language is responsible for defining a log density function conditioned on data", and that Stan "only requires models to be defined up to a constant that does not depend on the parameters" (§7.3, p. 77). Carpenter et al. (2017, §4, p. 16) add that the program's execution "details are the same whether a Stan program is being used by one of the built-in samplers or optimizers or being used externally by a user-defined sampler or optimizer", because other algorithms are implemented "using the log density and derivative functions supplied by a model" (§7.1, p. 26). A Stan program declares one mathematical object; the sampler is chosen separately and can be exchanged. Bandali (2020, §1.2, p. 3) states the same separation as a rule of method: "tool support and the analysis performance of those tools are subject to change, and can change much more easily than the logic and semantics of the language".

    In the Bellman calculus the separation is the distinction between Υ, the mathematical interpretation of a stage file, and Ρ, its numerical realization. The entry semantics states it: "The mathematical model specifies domains, equations, shock probability distributions and feasible choices. A numerical method supplies a procedure and representations intended to approximate or compute that model." Parameters belong to Υ and define the mathematical problem; settings belong to Ρ and configure its approximation. A method tag such as !egm "selects a numerical technique for these equations; it supplies no missing condition" (Methodization).

    Meaning assigned part by part¶

    A text that appeals to no order of execution must take its meaning from the mathematical objects its parts denote. Scott and Strachey (1971, p. 1) draw the founding distinction: "The numerals are expressions in a certain familiar language; while the numbers are mathematical objects (abstract objects) which provide the intended interpretations of the expressions." Schmidt (1986, Introduction, p. 1) separates syntax, "the appearance and structure of its sentences", from semantics, "the assignment of meanings to the sentences", and from pragmatics, "the usability of the language". The assignment is compositional when, in the words of Winskel (1993, §5.2, p. 60), "the denotation of a command is constructed from the denotations of its immediate subcommands", a property Winskel calls "a hallmark of denotational semantics" and one that fails for the operational semantics of a while-loop. Hutton (2023, §3, p. 3) states the consequence: "the meaning of a compound term is defined purely in terms of the meaning of its subterms. Compositionality aids understanding by ensuring that the semantics is modular and supports the use of simple equational reasoning techniques." Goguen, Thatcher, Wagner and Wright (1977, §3.2, p. 76) give the algebraic form: a grammar "yields an initial many-sorted algebra", "semantics arises from an algebra of the same type, and 'meaning' is the unique homomorphism" (see initial algebra).

    None of these authors discusses declarativeness; the connection is this entry's reading, and the reader should weigh it as such. Winskel's remark supplies the connection: a compositional meaning has to be constructed from the parts of the text, and the one meaning that cannot be so constructed is the trace of a loop. In this repository Υ is the meaning map and the semgraph is its result; the fold of the statements of a block into the boxes of a builder is the construction by structural recursion.

    Compositional meaning is what licenses the substitution of equals for equals, the convention Stoy (1977, §1.1, pp. 5–7) calls "one particularly important convention in mathematics" and the entry referential transparency states for this project. Backus (1978, §11.4.4, p. 624) attributes the simplicity of his systems to "the most elementary fixed naming system (naming a function in a definition) with a simple fixed rule of substituting a function for its name". A stage file follows the same discipline: a function is defined once and its name is used in the statements.

    A matter of degree¶

    The Stan Reference Manual shows that "declarative" names a position on a scale, by contrasting Stan with BUGS: "Unlike programs in BUGS, the declarations and statements making up a Stan program are executed in the order in which they are written" (ch. 7, p. 72); "In BUGS, for loops are declarative, defining plates in directed graphical model notation, which can be thought of as repeated substructures in the graphical model. Therefore, it is illegal in BUGS or JAGS to have a for loop that repeatedly reassigns a value to a variable" (§7.7, pp. 89–90); and "Because Stan is an imperative language, print statements can be very useful for debugging" (§7.13, p. 99). Carpenter et al. (2017, §5.2, p. 22) say the same, and add that a sampling statement "is nothing more than shorthand for incrementing the log density accumulator" (§5.1, p. 21). The object a Stan program defines, a density, is declared; the text that defines it is an ordered sequence of statements. BUGS is declarative in both respects, at the cost recorded below. Dynare's .mod file, which the project's toolkit comparison describes as "a declarative .mod language" with a separate engine, is the other declarative tool most economists have used.

    The declarative statement and its realization¶

    A stage file may contain both a declaration and the lines of a method that realize it, and the mark !doc tells the two apart. The policy block of the consumption stage cons.bl states the choice as a maximization over the feasible set,

    c = argmax_{cp : Γ(m_d)}(𝑢(cp) + V[>])   :: [id=optimality, !doc]

    The mark !doc identifies the declarative statement of what the control is, the mathematical definition of the object; it is neither documentation to be skipped nor an implementation detail. The other lines of the block, the inverse Euler inequality with its clause, the endogenous-grid line and the identification c = c[>], are one realization of that statement by a method, the endogenous grid method. The file thus reads as the economist writes the problem, a maximization subject to a feasibility constraint, with the numerical method's lines recognizable as a realization of it. The card of the policy builder prints the !doc line under Declared and the other lines as the builder's operations (The builders graphs §3; A builder §2 for the operations): the !doc line forms no operation, and the feasible set its binder declares enters the type of the policy.

    The mark draws the Stan and BUGS contrast inside one file: the statement marked !doc declares what the control is, and the other statements of the block record one way to compute it. An argmax line written without !doc is itself operative: it forms a row of kind Argmax, whose output is the set of maximizing policies, and a numerical method bound to the row makes the selection (operations).

    What declaring does not settle¶

    Declaring a meaning leaves the behavior of a computation open, in two directions. First, two texts with one denotation can compute differently. The Stan Reference Manual (§6.12, pp. 70–71), on derivatives taken statement by statement through an expression of the form x − x, warns: "Rather than the x − x canceling out, it introduces a 0 into the numerator and denominator of the chain-rule evaluation. The only way to avoid this kind problem is to be careful to do the necessary algebraic reductions as part of the model". In the calculus this is a fact about Ρ, not Υ, and the entry referential transparency makes the same distinction: replacing exact expectation by a quadrature approximation "requires an error argument rather than an appeal to equality". Two realizations of one declared stage need not agree numerically, and nothing in the declaration guarantees that they do.

    Second, a language that is declarative throughout forbids constructions that some computations use; the BUGS prohibition on reassignment quoted above is the example. Naish (1996, §9.3, pp. 14–15) records a related gap, between equality as written and equality in the intended interpretation: "there is nothing to prevent Prolog from attempting to unify these two terms and failing, even though they are equal in this interpretation."

    The declared artifact and its reading¶

    Declaring requires an artifact that can be read. Pfenning (2021, §1, p. L11.1) defines elaboration as "a translation mediating between specific forms of concrete syntax and internal representation in abstract syntax"; in this project the word names the step that reads the stage files and constructs the semgraph, and the entry syntax separates the concrete syntax a modeler writes from the abstract syntax that records its structure. Oliveira and Löh (2012, §2.1–2.2, pp. 2–3) name the price of writing a language directly as code in a host language: "shallow embeddings come at a price. We are committing to a specific semantics — in this case, evaluation." The project's review of carrier formats makes the same division, between languages with their own grammar, "Dynare .mod, dyno .dyno, Stan, Modelica, gEcon .gcn", and tools embedded in a host language, "JuMP, Pyomo/CVXPY, SSJ blocks, HARK objects, POMDPs.jl", which offer "free syntax and tooling, but no declarative artifact — nothing for Υ to read." The toolkit comparison records in the same spirit that QuantEcon "is a LIBRARY of numerical primitives, not a model-specification language". A stage file kept as abstract syntax can be read by more than one interpretation, of which the semgraph is the first.

    Where the design looks procedural¶

    Three features of the design look procedural; the documents state the following about each.

    1. The order of the bindings. A stage version is the stage occurrence as it stands after elaboration or after one of its three bindings, the calibration, the methods and the settings. Methodization precedes configuration, because the methods name the settings, and calibration may come at any point; each binding adds to the stage occurrence it is called on and returns that stage (The binding verbs §§1 and 3; specified stage).
    2. Statement order within a block. The boxes of a builder are emitted in the order the terms nest them, and a builder's card lists its operations in the order of application, each after every operation whose output it reads (operations §1).
    3. Method names in outer files. A methods file names algorithms such as the endogenous grid method. It names computations that the stage file created and creates none, and a method tag supplies no missing condition (Methodization §§1 and 2), which is the declarative reading this entry gives.

    Related concepts¶

    • Bellman-SYM: the language whose declarative character this entry defines.
    • semgraph and Υ — meaning construction: the meaning a stage file is assigned, and the map that assigns it.
    • Ρ — numerical representation and methodization: the numerical realization supplied separately, and the step that names it.
    • semantics: the distinction between interpretation, representation and realization.
    • syntax and statement: the concrete and abstract forms of what is declared, and the !doc mark.
    • Referential transparency and Functional programming and FFP: substitution of equals, and Backus's functional style.
    • Initial algebra: the algebraic form of compositional meaning.
    • Specified stage: the stage after methods, settings and parameter values are attached.
    • Workflow §1: the workflow in which the declaration is the first step.
